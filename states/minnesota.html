Ramps & home modification

Programs that build, lend or pay for ramps, bathroom changes and other home access work.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • Whether you own or rent. If you rent, ask the landlord in writing before any work.
  • Photos of the entrance and a count of the steps, or the height from the ground to the door.
  • What the person uses to get around: wheelchair, scooter, walker or cane.
  • Household size and income, if the program is based on income.

Ask

  • Does the work have to be approved before it starts?
  • Is this a grant, a loan or a free build by volunteers?
  • Who handles permits and inspections?
  • Is there a waiting list, and how long is it?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Disability parking placard in Minnesota: how to apply

For: Mobility or physical disability

Minnesota Department of Public Safety, Driver and Vehicle Services (DVS)

In Minnesota, disability parking placards are issued by the Minnesota Department of Public Safety, Driver and Vehicle Services (DVS). Apply with the Application for Disability Parking Certificate (PS2005). Who can certify the disability: a licensed physician, physician assistant, advanced practice registered nurse, chiropractor or physical therapist.

Next step: Open the agency’s page for the current form and have the medical part completed. Apply online, in person at a deputy registrar office, or by mail to Driver and Vehicle Services (the address is on the form).

Good to know
  • Temporary certificates last 1 to 6 months and short-term certificates 7 to 12 months.
  • Temporary and short-term certificates cost $5; long-term (13 to 71 months) and permanent (six-year) certificates are free. Renewing a permanent certificate does not need a new medical signature.
  • A temporary certificate can also be issued for a pregnancy-related condition that makes walking unsafe.
  • Forms, fees and rules change. Check the agency’s page before you apply.

USDA Rural Development home repair loans and grants (Minnesota)

USDA Rural Development, St. Paul state office

The USDA Section 504 Home Repair program gives loans to low-income and very-low-income homeowners in eligible rural areas to repair, improve or modernize their homes, and grants to very-low-income homeowners age 62 and older to remove health and safety hazards. Repairs and improvements can sometimes include accessibility changes such as ramps or bathroom work, so ask the local office what qualifies.

Next step: Call the Minnesota Rural Development state office, ask for Single Family Housing, and ask which local office takes 504 repair applications for your county.

Good to know
  • Homeowners only; the home must be in an eligible rural area and household income must be low or very low for the county.
  • Grants are for homeowners age 62 and older with very low income; funding is limited each federal fiscal year.

Statewide

Section 811 Project Rental Assistance in Minnesota

Minnesota Housing, with the Minnesota Department of Human Services

Minnesota’s Section 811 program serves single adults and families that include an adult with a disability who is at least 18 and under 62 at move-in, who is eligible for community-based long-term services paid by Medicaid or the state (such as waiver services, adult mental health services or Money Follows the Person), and whose household income is at or below HUD’s extremely low income limit. It focuses on people experiencing long-term homelessness with serious mental illness who are in the PATH outreach program, and on people leaving an institution after 60 days or more through Money Follows the Person.

Next step: Ask the agency that serves you, such as your PATH outreach worker or Money Follows the Person transition coordinator, to refer you. A state housing coordinator keeps the statewide waiting list.

Good to know
  • You cannot refer yourself; an agency that works with the target groups must refer you.
  • If no one from the target groups is waiting for a site, other eligible people may be offered a unit.

Early intervention for babies and toddlers (birth to age 3)

For: Children, youth and families

Department of Children, Youth, and Families; Minnesota Department of Education

The early intervention program in Minnesota serves children from birth to age three who have a developmental delay or disability. Parents do not have to wait for a referral, and the evaluation is free.

Next step: Call the referral line. Say you are concerned about your child’s development and want an evaluation under Part C of IDEA. Write down the name of each person you speak with and the date.

Good to know
  • For children under age three. If your child is three or older, ask your school district for a special education evaluation, which is also free.
  • The evaluation and service coordination are free. Depending on state policy, some services may have a sliding-scale fee. A child cannot be denied services because the family cannot pay.
  • For an eligible child, the steps from referral to a written service plan (an Individualized Family Service Plan) should take 45 days.
  • This is the office the ECTA Center, a technical assistance center funded by the U.S. Department of Education, lists for Minnesota under Part C of the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA). The points here about referral, cost and timing come from the Center for Parent Information and Resources.
