Ramps & home modification

Programs that build, lend or pay for ramps, bathroom changes and other home access work.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • Whether you own or rent. If you rent, ask the landlord in writing before any work.
  • Photos of the entrance and a count of the steps, or the height from the ground to the door.
  • What the person uses to get around: wheelchair, scooter, walker or cane.
  • Household size and income, if the program is based on income.

Ask

  • Does the work have to be approved before it starts?
  • Is this a grant, a loan or a free build by volunteers?
  • Who handles permits and inspections?
  • Is there a waiting list, and how long is it?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • The make, model and serial number of the equipment, if you have them.
  • Who paid for it and when: Medicare, Medicaid, the VA, private insurance or you.
  • The name of the supplier or dealer, and any warranty papers.
  • What is wrong, when it started and what it keeps you from doing.

Ask

  • Can I borrow or try equipment while I wait?
  • Is there a cost, and can it be reduced or waived?
  • How long is the wait, and who do I call to check on it?
  • Do you serve my county?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Disability parking placard in the District of Columbia: how to apply

For: Mobility or physical disability

DC Department of Motor Vehicles (for the District Department of Transportation)

In the District of Columbia, disability parking placards are issued by the DC Department of Motor Vehicles (for the District Department of Transportation). Apply with the Application for Disability Parking Placard or Tags (also available in Spanish and other languages). Who can certify the disability: a medical practitioner; you may self-certify in person if you are missing a lower extremity or use a motorized wheelchair.

Next step: Open the agency’s page for the current form and have the medical part completed. Apply online, fax to (202) 673-9908, mail to DC DMV, Attn: Disability Services, P.O. Box 90120, Washington, DC 20090, or apply in person.

Good to know
  • A one-week permit is available without a doctor’s certification.
  • There is no charge for a placard or permit.
  • Long-term placards are valid up to eight years.
  • Forms, fees and rules change. Check the agency’s page before you apply.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • Your Social Security number and a photo ID.
  • Names, addresses and dates for your doctors, clinics and hospitals, and a list of your medicines.
  • Recent pay stubs, benefit letters and bank statements.
  • Any denial or decision letter. Note the date on it, because appeal deadlines are counted from the day you receive it.

Ask

  • Which program fits my situation, and can I apply for more than one?
  • What is the deadline to appeal, and where do I send the appeal?
  • Will working or saving money change my benefits?
  • Who can help me fill out the forms at no cost?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Have ready

Early intervention for babies and toddlers (birth to age 3)

For: Children, youth and families

Office of the State Superintendent of Education (OSSE); Strong Start DC Early Intervention Program (DCEIP); Division of Early Learning - Government of the District of Columbia

The early intervention program in the District of Columbia serves children from birth to age three who have a developmental delay or disability. Parents do not have to wait for a referral, and the evaluation is free.

Next step: Call the referral line. Say you are concerned about your child’s development and want an evaluation under Part C of IDEA. Write down the name of each person you speak with and the date.

Good to know
  • For children under age three. If your child is three or older, ask your school district for a special education evaluation, which is also free.
  • The evaluation and service coordination are free. Depending on state policy, some services may have a sliding-scale fee. A child cannot be denied services because the family cannot pay.
  • For an eligible child, the steps from referral to a written service plan (an Individualized Family Service Plan) should take 45 days.
  • This is the office the ECTA Center, a technical assistance center funded by the U.S. Department of Education, lists for the District of Columbia under Part C of the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA). The points here about referral, cost and timing come from the Center for Parent Information and Resources.

Vision, hearing & communication

Services for people who are blind, have low vision, are deaf, hard of hearing or DeafBlind: training, reading services, phone equipment and relay.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • A recent eye exam or hearing test, if you have one.
  • What has become hard to do: reading mail, using the phone, getting around, working.
  • Proof of where you live and of income, if the program asks.
  • How you want to be contacted: voice, text, email, videophone or relay.

Ask

  • Do you come to my home, or do I travel to you?
  • Is the equipment free, loaned or sold?
  • Who can sign the form that confirms my vision or hearing loss?
  • Is there training for my family too?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

DC Department of Health, Bureau of Epidemiology and Health Risk

DC Traumatic Brain Injury Program is run by or through DC Department of Health, Bureau of Epidemiology and Health Risk, which the National Association of State Head Injury Administrators lists as the state lead on brain injury. State brain injury programs coordinate services, trust fund or waiver supports, public information and the state advisory council for people with traumatic or acquired brain injury, including veterans and people hurt in crashes, falls or sports.

Next step: Contact the program and ask about brain injury resource facilitation, any trust fund or waiver services you may qualify for, and local support groups.

Good to know
  • Services and eligibility vary a lot by state; many are funded by trust funds or Medicaid waivers with limited slots.

Georgetown UCEDD/LEND

For: Intellectual or developmental disability

Georgetown University

Georgetown UCEDD/LEND is the federally funded University Center for Excellence in Developmental Disabilities (UCEDD) at Georgetown University. UCEDDs are required to serve every state and work with people with developmental disabilities, their families, and community agencies through training, information and referral, technical assistance, research and direct community projects such as assistive technology, transition, and employment support.

Next step: Call the center and ask what community projects, assistive technology help, or referrals it offers for adults and children with developmental disabilities in your area.

Good to know
  • Services vary by center and funding; many UCEDDs focus on training, research and referral rather than ongoing direct care.

Rights, advocacy & legal help

Disability rights agencies, complaint offices and legal aid for denials, access barriers, school, health care and voting.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • What happened, where, when and who was involved, written down.
  • Copies of letters, emails, notices and photos.
  • What you asked for and the answer you got.
  • The date on any denial or notice. Deadlines can be short.

Ask

  • Does a law cover this, and which office enforces it?
  • What is my deadline to complain or appeal?
  • Can you represent me, or only advise me?
  • What should I put in writing right now?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Statewide

District of Columbia State Long-Term Care Ombudsman

Legal Counsel for the Elderly

Every state has an Office of the State Long-Term Care Ombudsman. Ombudsmen are free, confidential advocates for people living in nursing homes, assisted living and other care facilities. They take complaints about care, rights, discharge and billing and work to resolve them at the resident's direction. Family members, friends and visiting volunteers can call too.

Next step: Call the state ombudsman office and ask to be connected to the local ombudsman for the facility where the resident lives.

Good to know
  • The ombudsman works for the resident and cannot take sides with the facility or family; services are free and confidential.

Statewide

Mid-Atlantic ADA Center

TransCen, Inc.

The Mid-Atlantic ADA Center is the regional ADA Center for this state in the ADA National Network. It answers questions about the Americans with Disabilities Act, including access to buildings, programs, transportation and employment, and it gives guidance and training. It is hosted by TransCen, Inc..

Next step: Call or use the website and ask your ADA question; the center can explain the law and your options, but it does not take legal cases.

Good to know
  • ADA Centers give information and guidance; they are not law firms and do not enforce the ADA.
  • The number serves your whole region, not only this state.

District of Columbia Adult Protective Services: report abuse, neglect or exploitation

District of Columbia Adult Protective Services

The reporting line in District of Columbia for suspected abuse, neglect or financial exploitation of older adults and adults with disabilities. Adult Protective Services takes the report, decides whether it meets the state’s rules and looks into it. You can make a report without giving your name.

DC Department of Behavioral Health: state mental health agency for the District of Columbia

For: Mental health condition

DC Department of Behavioral Health

SAMHSA lists this as the state mental health agency for the District of Columbia. Each state’s agency oversees mental health treatment programs, and according to SAMHSA it can give more information about treatment services offered in the state and about how treatment facilities are licensed.

Next step: Call or visit the agency’s website and ask how to find public mental health services where you live, such as a community mental health center, and what help there is if you have no insurance or are on Medicaid.

Good to know
  • In a mental health crisis, call or text 988, or chat at 988lifeline.org. You do not need to be suicidal to reach out.

Developmental disability agencies

County boards, regional centers and state offices for developmental disabilities. They decide eligibility, coordinate services and manage waiver funding.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • Records that show when the disability began, such as school or medical evaluations.
  • Proof of where you live.
  • A Medicaid card, if you have one.

Ask

  • How do I apply for eligibility, and how long does it take?
  • Is there a waiting list for waiver services?
  • Who will be my service coordinator?
  • What help can I get while I wait?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.
