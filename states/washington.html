Ramps & home modification

Programs that build, lend or pay for ramps, bathroom changes and other home access work.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • Whether you own or rent. If you rent, ask the landlord in writing before any work.
  • Photos of the entrance and a count of the steps, or the height from the ground to the door.
  • What the person uses to get around: wheelchair, scooter, walker or cane.
  • Household size and income, if the program is based on income.

Ask

  • Does the work have to be approved before it starts?
  • Is this a grant, a loan or a free build by volunteers?
  • Who handles permits and inspections?
  • Is there a waiting list, and how long is it?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Disability parking placard in Washington: how to apply

For: Mobility or physical disability

Washington State Department of Licensing (DOL)

In Washington, disability parking placards are issued by the Washington State Department of Licensing (DOL). Apply with the Disabled Parking Application for Individuals, plus a separate signed prescription or authorization from your provider. Who can certify the disability: a medical doctor, osteopath, chiropractor, podiatrist, naturopath, advanced registered nurse practitioner or physician assistant.

Next step: Open the agency’s page for the current form and have the medical part completed. Bring the application to a vehicle licensing office or mail it to Department of Licensing, PO Box 9043, Olympia, WA 98507. Temporary applications can also be submitted online.

Good to know
  • A temporary (red) placard is valid for up to one year and has no fee.
  • Placards are free. Permanent privileges last five years and need a new prescription to renew.
  • Washington’s qualifying conditions include acute sensitivity to vehicle emissions and porphyria. Veterans rated 70% or higher who use a service animal can apply with a separate veteran form.
  • Forms, fees and rules change. Check the agency’s page before you apply.

USDA Rural Development home repair loans and grants (Washington)

USDA Rural Development, Olympia state office

The USDA Section 504 Home Repair program gives loans to low-income and very-low-income homeowners in eligible rural areas to repair, improve or modernize their homes, and grants to very-low-income homeowners age 62 and older to remove health and safety hazards. Repairs and improvements can sometimes include accessibility changes such as ramps or bathroom work, so ask the local office what qualifies.

Next step: Call the Washington Rural Development state office, ask for Single Family Housing, and ask which local office takes 504 repair applications for your county.

Good to know
  • Homeowners only; the home must be in an eligible rural area and household income must be low or very low for the county.
  • Grants are for homeowners age 62 and older with very low income; funding is limited each federal fiscal year.

For: Children, youth and families

Department of Children, Youth and Families; Early Support for Infants and Toddlers Program

The early intervention program in Washington serves children from birth to age three who have a developmental delay or disability. Parents do not have to wait for a referral, and the evaluation is free.

  • This is the office the ECTA Center, a technical assistance center funded by the U.S. Department of Education, lists for Washington under Part C of the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA). For: Blind or low vision, Older adults

Washington State Department of Services for the Blind (Independent Living Program)

This is Washington’s program under the federal Independent Living Services for Older Individuals who are Blind (OIB) grant, run by the Washington State Department of Services for the Blind (Independent Living Program). According to Time To Be Bold, these services often include training in making the most of remaining vision, using magnifiers and other devices, using phones and computers, staying safe and organized at home and getting around the community. Washington Office of the State Long-Term Care Ombudsman

Every state has an Office of the State Long-Term Care Ombudsman. Ombudsmen are free, confidential advocates for people living in nursing homes, assisted living and other care facilities. They take complaints about care, rights, discharge and billing and work to resolve them at the resident's direction. Family members, friends and visiting volunteers can call too.

Next step: Call the state ombudsman office and ask to be connected to the local ombudsman for the facility where the resident lives.

Good to know
  • The ombudsman works for the resident and cannot take sides with the facility or family; services are free and confidential.
