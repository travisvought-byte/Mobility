Ramps & home modification

Programs that build, lend or pay for ramps, bathroom changes and other home access work.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • Whether you own or rent. If you rent, ask the landlord in writing before any work.
  • Photos of the entrance and a count of the steps, or the height from the ground to the door.
  • What the person uses to get around: wheelchair, scooter, walker or cane.
  • Household size and income, if the program is based on income.

Ask

  • Does the work have to be approved before it starts?
  • Is this a grant, a loan or a free build by volunteers?
  • Who handles permits and inspections?
  • Is there a waiting list, and how long is it?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Equipment, repair & assistive technology

Try, borrow, repair, finance or reuse wheelchairs and assistive technology.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • The make, model and serial number of the equipment, if you have them.
  • Who paid for it and when: Medicare, Medicaid, the VA, private insurance or you.
  • The name of the supplier or dealer, and any warranty papers.
  • What is wrong, when it started and what it keeps you from doing.

Ask

  • Can I borrow or try equipment while I wait?
  • Is there a cost, and can it be reduced or waived?
  • How long is the wait, and who do I call to check on it?
  • Do you serve my county?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Disability parking placard in Iowa: how to apply

For: Mobility or physical disability

Iowa Department of Transportation (Iowa DOT)

In Iowa, disability parking placards are issued by the Iowa Department of Transportation (Iowa DOT). Apply with the Application for Persons with Disabilities Parking Permit for Iowa Residents. Who can certify the disability: a health care provider, who completes a medical statement (veterans can use their U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs certification instead).

Next step: Open the agency’s page for the current form and have the medical part completed. Apply online and upload the medical statement; mail the form and statement to Iowa DOT Vehicle Services, P.O. Box 9278, Des Moines, IA 50306-9278; or bring them to any Iowa DMV or your county treasurer’s office.

Good to know
  • Iowa also offers disability license plates and a disability sticker for a regular plate.
  • Forms, fees and rules change. Check the agency’s page before you apply.

Iowa Department of Transportation: rural public transit program (Section 5311)

Iowa Department of Transportation

The Iowa Department of Transportation runs the federal Section 5311 program, which funds public transit in towns and rural areas with fewer than 50,000 people. Rural transit systems in this state are paid for in part through this office, and many offer rides that people with disabilities can use.

Income, benefits & health coverage

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • Your Social Security number and a photo ID.
  • Names, addresses and dates for your doctors, clinics and hospitals, and a list of your medicines.
  • Recent pay stubs, benefit letters and bank statements.
  • Any denial or decision letter. Note the date on it, because appeal deadlines are counted from the day you receive it.

Ask

  • Which program fits my situation, and can I apply for more than one?
  • What is the deadline to appeal, and where do I send the appeal?
  • Will working or saving money change my benefits?
  • Who can help me fill out the forms at no cost?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

USDA Rural Development home repair loans and grants (Iowa)

USDA Rural Development, Des Moines state office

The USDA Section 504 Home Repair program gives loans to low-income and very-low-income homeowners in eligible rural areas to repair, improve or modernize their homes, and grants to very-low-income homeowners age 62 and older to remove health and safety hazards. Repairs and improvements can sometimes include accessibility changes such as ramps or bathroom work, so ask the local office what qualifies.

Next step: Call the Iowa Rural Development state office, ask for Single Family Housing, and ask which local office takes 504 repair applications for your county.

Good to know
  • Homeowners only; the home must be in an eligible rural area and household income must be low or very low for the county.
  • Grants are for homeowners age 62 and older with very low income; funding is limited each federal fiscal year.

In-home help, food & daily living

Help at home, paid family caregiving, breaks for caregivers, food benefits, help paying heating and cooling bills, and a discount on phone or internet service.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • A list of what you need help with each day, such as bathing, meals or getting out of bed.
  • Your Medicaid card, if you have one.
  • Proof of income and household size.
  • For the phone or internet discount: proof that you get SSI, Medicaid or SNAP.

Ask

  • Can a family member or friend be paid to help me?
  • How many hours of help can I get, and who decides?
  • Is there a waiting list?
  • What do I do if my helper does not show up?
  • For energy bill help: when are applications taken, and is there faster help if I have a shut-off notice?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • Photo ID and Social Security numbers for everyone in the household.
  • Proof of income and benefits.
  • Your lease, and any eviction or rent increase notice.

Ask

  • Is the waiting list open, and is there a preference for people with disabilities?
  • Can I apply to more than one housing agency?
  • How do I ask for a reasonable accommodation?
  • Who can help me while I wait?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Statewide

Early intervention for babies and toddlers (birth to age 3)

For: Children, youth and families

Early ACCESS (IDEA/Part C); Iowa Department of Education; Bureau of Early Childhood Services

The early intervention program in Iowa serves children from birth to age three who have a developmental delay or disability. Parents do not have to wait for a referral, and the evaluation is free.

Next step: Call the referral line. Say you are concerned about your child’s development and want an evaluation under Part C of IDEA. Write down the name of each person you speak with and the date.

Good to know
  • For children under age three. If your child is three or older, ask your school district for a special education evaluation, which is also free.
  • The evaluation and service coordination are free. Depending on state policy, some services may have a sliding-scale fee. A child cannot be denied services because the family cannot pay.
  • For an eligible child, the steps from referral to a written service plan (an Individualized Family Service Plan) should take 45 days.
  • This is the office the ECTA Center, a technical assistance center funded by the U.S. Department of Education, lists for Iowa under Part C of the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA). The points here about referral, cost and timing come from the Center for Parent Information and Resources.

Vision, hearing & communication

Services for people who are blind, have low vision, are deaf, hard of hearing or DeafBlind: training, reading services, phone equipment and relay.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • A recent eye exam or hearing test, if you have one.
  • What has become hard to do: reading mail, using the phone, getting around, working.
  • Proof of where you live and of income, if the program asks.

Ask

  • Do you come to my home, or do I travel to you?
  • Is the equipment free, loaned or sold?
  • Who can sign the form that confirms my vision or hearing loss?
  • Is there training for my family too?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Independent living centers

Disability-led centers that help with equipment, home access, peer support and advocacy.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • A short list of what you want to change: housing, equipment, work or moving out of a nursing home.
  • Your county, and how you want to be contacted.

Ask

  • Which office serves my county?
  • Can I talk with a peer who has been through this?
  • Can you help me apply for programs or appeal a denial?
  • Do you lend equipment, or know who does?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Centers for Independent Living in Iowa

Federally funded Centers for Independent Living

Disability-led centers that help with equipment, home access, peer support, advocacy and moving from a facility back to the community. Federally funded centers listed for Iowa: Disabilities Resource Center of Siouxland (Sioux City, 712-255-1065); South Central Iowa Center for Independent Living (Oskaloosa, 641-672-1867); Access 2 Independence (Iowa City, 319-338-3870); Central Iowa Center for Independent Living (Des Moines, 515-243-1742); League of Human Dignity (Council Bluffs, 712-323-6863); Illinois/Iowa Center for Independent Living (Rock Island, 309-793-0090).

Next step: Call the center nearest you, say what you need (ramp, equipment, peer support) and ask which counties it serves.

Good to know
  • Service areas differ by center; confirm that yours covers your county.
  • Federal list covers Part C centers only; some states also have other centers.

Statewide

Iowa Statewide Independent Living Council

Each state has a Statewide Independent Living Council that works with the state's Centers for Independent Living and guides the state plan for independent living services. It is a planning and advocacy council, not a direct-service center, but its staff can point you to the center that serves your county.

Next step: Contact the council and ask which Center for Independent Living serves your county.

Good to know
  • Planning and advocacy council; direct services come from the Centers for Independent Living.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

Ask

  • What will they ask me for?
  • Is there someone who can help me apply?

Iowa Council on Developmental Disabilities

For: Intellectual or developmental disability

Every state has a governor-appointed Council on Developmental Disabilities, made up mostly of people with developmental disabilities and their family members. It works on policy, advocacy, training and grants rather than direct services, but its staff can point you to the right state agency, local board and self-advocacy group.

Next step: Call the council and ask where to apply for developmental disability services and which self-advocacy groups are near you.

Good to know
  • Policy and advocacy council; it does not run intake or provide direct services.

Statewide

Iowa's University Center for Excellence in Disabilities

For: Intellectual or developmental disability

University of Iowa

Iowa's University Center for Excellence in Disabilities is the federally funded University Center for Excellence in Developmental Disabilities (UCEDD) at University of Iowa. UCEDDs are required to serve every state and work with people with developmental disabilities, their families, and community agencies through training, information and referral, technical assistance, research and direct community projects such as assistive technology, transition, and employment support.

Next step: Call the center and ask what community projects, assistive technology help, or referrals it offers for adults and children with developmental disabilities in your area.

Good to know
  • Services vary by center and funding; many UCEDDs focus on training, research and referral rather than ongoing direct care.

Statewide

Iowa Brain Injury Services Program

For: Brain injury or stroke

Iowa Department of Health and Human Services

Iowa Brain Injury Services Program is run by or through Iowa Department of Health and Human Services, which the National Association of State Head Injury Administrators lists as the state lead on brain injury. State brain injury programs coordinate services, trust fund or waiver supports, public information and the state advisory council for people with traumatic or acquired brain injury, including veterans and people hurt in crashes, falls or sports. The statewide advocacy group is the Brain Injury Association of Iowa.

Next step: Contact the program and ask about brain injury resource facilitation, any trust fund or waiver services you may qualify for, and local support groups.

Good to know
  • Services and eligibility vary a lot by state; many are funded by trust funds or Medicaid waivers with limited slots.

Rights, advocacy & legal help

Disability rights agencies, complaint offices and legal aid for denials, access barriers, school, health care and voting.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • What happened, where, when and who was involved, written down.
  • Copies of letters, emails, notices and photos.
  • What you asked for and the answer you got.
  • The date on any denial or notice. Deadlines can be short.

Ask

  • Does a law cover this, and which office enforces it?
  • What is my deadline to complain or appeal?
  • Can you represent me, or only advise me?
  • What should I put in writing right now?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Statewide

Great Plains ADA Center

University of Missouri

The Great Plains ADA Center is the regional ADA Center for this state in the ADA National Network. It answers questions about the Americans with Disabilities Act, including access to buildings, programs, transportation and employment, and it gives guidance and training. It is hosted by University of Missouri.

Next step: Call or use the website and ask your ADA question; the center can explain the law and your options, but it does not take legal cases.

Good to know
  • ADA Centers give information and guidance; they are not law firms and do not enforce the ADA.
  • The number serves your whole region, not only this state.

Statewide

Iowa State Long-Term Care Ombudsman

Iowa Department on Aging

Every state has an Office of the State Long-Term Care Ombudsman. Ombudsmen are free, confidential advocates for people living in nursing homes, assisted living and other care facilities. They take complaints about care, rights, discharge and billing and work to resolve them at the resident's direction. Family members, friends and visiting volunteers can call too.

Next step: Call the state ombudsman office and ask to be connected to the local ombudsman for the facility where the resident lives.

Good to know
  • The ombudsman works for the resident and cannot take sides with the facility or family; services are free and confidential.

Crisis, safety & mental health

Lines to call or text right now, how to report abuse or neglect, and mental health support.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • Where you are right now, in case help needs to find you.
  • For a report about someone else: who is at risk, where they live and what you saw or were told.
  • A safe phone number or email where they can reach you.

Ask

  • What happens after this call?
  • Can I use text, relay, a videophone or an interpreter instead of a voice call?
  • Is there a case or reference number I should keep?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Statewide

Iowa Dependent Adult Abuse Program: report abuse, neglect or exploitation

Iowa Dependent Adult Abuse Program

The reporting line in Iowa for suspected abuse, neglect or financial exploitation of older adults and adults with disabilities. Adult Protective Services takes the report, decides whether it meets the state’s rules and looks into it. You can make a report without giving your name.

Next step: Call and say who is at risk, where they are and what you have seen or been told. Ask what happens next and whether you can get an update.

Good to know
  • In immediate danger, call 911.

Statewide

Iowa Department of Health and Human Services: state mental health agency for Iowa

For: Mental health condition

Iowa Department of Health and Human Services

SAMHSA lists this as the state mental health agency for Iowa. Each state’s agency oversees mental health treatment programs, and according to SAMHSA it can give more information about treatment services offered in the state and about how treatment facilities are licensed.

Next step: Call or visit the agency’s website and ask how to find public mental health services where you live, such as a community mental health center, and what help there is if you have no insurance or are on Medicaid.

Good to know
  • In a mental health crisis, call or text 988, or chat at 988lifeline.org. You do not need to be suicidal to reach out.

Developmental disability agencies

County boards, regional centers and state offices for developmental disabilities. They decide eligibility, coordinate services and manage waiver funding.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • Records that show when the disability began, such as school or medical evaluations.
  • Proof of where you live.
  • A Medicaid card, if you have one.

Ask

  • How do I apply for eligibility, and how long does it take?
  • Is there a waiting list for waiver services?
  • Who will be my service coordinator?
  • What help can I get while I wait?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.
