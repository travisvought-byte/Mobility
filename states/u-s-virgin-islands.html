Equipment, repair & assistive technology

Try, borrow, repair, finance or reuse wheelchairs and assistive technology.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • The make, model and serial number of the equipment, if you have them.
  • Who paid for it and when: Medicare, Medicaid, the VA, private insurance or you.
  • The name of the supplier or dealer, and any warranty papers.
  • What is wrong, when it started and what it keeps you from doing.

Ask

  • Can I borrow or try equipment while I wait?
  • Is there a cost, and can it be reduced or waived?
  • How long is the wait, and who do I call to check on it?
  • Do you serve my county?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Serves all of U.S. Virgin Islands

U.S. Virgin Islands assistive technology program (Technology Related Assistance for Individuals with Disabilities)

Assistive Technology Act program

The Assistive Technology Act program for the U.S. Virgin Islands. The federal program gives one grant to each state and territory to help people with disabilities of all ages try, borrow, reuse and pay for assistive technology.

Next step: Call and ask which devices you can try or borrow, whether reused equipment is available and what help there is to pay for a device.

Good to know
  • Availability varies. Ask what is in stock before you travel.

Income, benefits & health coverage

Social Security disability, Medicaid, Medicare counseling, ABLE savings accounts and help appealing a denial.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • Your Social Security number and a photo ID.
  • Names, addresses and dates for your doctors, clinics and hospitals, and a list of your medicines.
  • Recent pay stubs, benefit letters and bank statements.
  • Any denial or decision letter. Note the date on it, because appeal deadlines are counted from the day you receive it.

Ask

  • Which program fits my situation, and can I apply for more than one?
  • What is the deadline to appeal, and where do I send the appeal?
  • Will working or saving money change my benefits?
  • Who can help me fill out the forms at no cost?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

U.S. Virgin Islands Department of Human Services, Office of Medicaid

This is the contact Medicaid.gov lists for the U.S. Virgin Islands to apply for Medicaid or CHIP, check your eligibility or an application, replace a card or find a provider.

Next step: Call or open the website and say you want to apply for Medicaid, or check on an application. Ask what services are covered and how to appeal if you are turned down.

Good to know
  • Eligibility rules and covered services are set locally; ask what is covered.

Housing help

Rental help, housing vouchers and what to do about housing discrimination. For ramps and changes to a home you already have, see Ramps and home modification.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • Photo ID and Social Security numbers for everyone in the household.
  • Proof of income and benefits.
  • Your lease, and any eviction or rent increase notice.
  • A short list of the access features you need, such as no steps or a roll-in shower.

Ask

  • Is the waiting list open, and is there a preference for people with disabilities?
  • Can I apply to more than one housing agency?
  • How do I ask for a reasonable accommodation?
  • Who can help me while I wait?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Virgin Islands Housing Finance Authority

Virgin Islands Housing Finance Authority is listed by the National Council of State Housing Agencies as the U.S. Virgin Islands’s housing finance agency. Work, school & training

Vocational rehabilitation, job centers, job accommodations, federal hiring and discrimination charges. For a child’s school plan, see Help for a child.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • What kind of work or training you want, even if you are not sure yet.
  • Records that describe your disability, if you have them.
  • For a job problem: dates, names and copies of emails or letters.
  • The accommodations you need for an interview, a class or a visit to an office.

Ask

  • How do I apply, and how long until I am assigned a counselor?
  • Can you pay for equipment, training or changes to a vehicle that I need for work?
  • What is the deadline to file a complaint?
  • If I disagree with a decision, who can help me appeal?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Serves all of U.S. Virgin Islands

Virgin Islands Division of Disabilities and Vocational Rehabilitation Services

The vocational rehabilitation agency helps people with disabilities prepare for, find and keep work. When equipment is needed for a work goal it can pay for assistive technology and vehicle modifications, and in some places home modifications. Eligibility and the order of service are set locally.

Next step: Call to apply, and ask about assistive technology, vehicle modification and home modification funding before you buy anything.

Good to know
  • Services must connect to an employment goal.
  • Apply and get approval before buying equipment or starting modifications.
  • Some agencies keep a waitlist or order of selection.

Babies, children & school

Early intervention for babies and toddlers, and parent centers that explain evaluations, school plans and your family’s rights.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • Your child’s age and what you have noticed, in your own words.
  • The names of your child’s doctor and any therapists.
  • Any evaluation, service plan (IFSP), IEP or 504 plan you already have.
  • A notebook. Write down each person you talk to and the date.

Ask

  • How do I ask for an evaluation, and how long will it take?
  • Is there any cost to my family?
  • Who will be my contact person or service coordinator?
  • If I disagree with a decision, what are my options and who can help me?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Early intervention for babies and toddlers (birth to age 3)

For: Children, youth and families

Infant/Toddler Program; Department of Health

The early intervention program in the U.S. Virgin Islands serves children from birth to age three who have a developmental delay or disability. Parents do not have to wait for a referral, and the evaluation is free.

Next step: Call the program office, or use its website, and ask how to refer your child where you live. Say you are concerned about your child’s development and want an evaluation under Part C of IDEA. Write down the name of each person you speak with and the date.

Good to know
  • For children under age three. If your child is three or older, ask your school district for a special education evaluation, which is also free.
  • The evaluation and service coordination are free. Depending on state policy, some services may have a sliding-scale fee. A child cannot be denied services because the family cannot pay.
  • For an eligible child, the steps from referral to a written service plan (an Individualized Family Service Plan) should take 45 days.
  • This is the office the ECTA Center, a technical assistance center funded by the U.S. Department of Education, lists for the U.S. Virgin Islands under Part C of the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA). The points here about referral, cost and timing come from the Center for Parent Information and Resources.

Family Voices, U.S. Virgin Islands: Family-to-Family Health Information Center

For: Children, youth and families

Family-to-Family Health Information Center

A family-led center for families of children and youth with special health care needs in the U.S. Virgin Islands. Staff are family members who have been through it themselves. They help families understand health coverage, find services and connect with other parents. Help is free to families.

Next step: Call and say what your child needs and where you are stuck, such as an insurance denial, finding a specialist or planning the move to adult health care. Ask to speak with a parent who has been through it.

Good to know
  • Family Voices lists this as a Family-to-Family Health Information Center for the U.S. Virgin Islands. The centers are funded by the federal Maternal and Child Health Bureau.

Virgin Islands Parent Training and Information Center (Disability Rights of the Virgin Islands): help for families of children with disabilities in the U.S. Virgin Islands

For: Children, youth and families

Virgin Islands Parent Training and Information Center (Disability Rights of the Virgin Islands)

Parent Centers are funded by the U.S. Department of Education under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) to give training and information to families of children and youth with disabilities, and every state has at least one. Listed by the Center for Parent Information and Resources as the Virgin Islands’ Parent Training and Information Center, run by Disability Rights of the Virgin Islands from its main office in Frederiksted, St. Croix.

Next step: Call or write and say what is happening with your child at school, such as an evaluation, an IEP or 504 plan, or a disagreement with the school. Ask for help understanding your rights and preparing for meetings.

Good to know
  • Help is generally free to families. Parent Centers give information and support, not legal representation.

Vision, hearing & communication

Services for people who are blind, have low vision, are deaf, hard of hearing or DeafBlind: training, reading services, phone equipment and relay.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • A recent eye exam or hearing test, if you have one.
  • What has become hard to do: reading mail, using the phone, getting around, working.
  • Proof of where you live and of income, if the program asks.
  • How you want to be contacted: voice, text, email, videophone or relay.

Ask

  • Do you come to my home, or do I travel to you?
  • Is the equipment free, loaned or sold?
  • Who can sign the form that confirms my vision or hearing loss?
  • Is there training for my family too?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Serves all of U.S. Virgin Islands

Independent living help for older adults with vision loss in the U.S. Virgin Islands

For: Blind or low vision, Older adults

Virgin Islands Department of Human Services, Division of Disabilities and Rehabilitation Services

The Virgin Islands Department of Human Services’ Division of Disabilities and Rehabilitation Services runs the federal independent living program for older people who are blind (OIB). It gives support services to people 55 and older whose severe visual impairment makes getting a job difficult but who can reach independent living goals.

Next step: Call the division and ask about independent living services for older adults who are blind or have low vision.

Good to know
  • Under the federal program these services are meant for people 55 and older who are not looking for work. If you want to keep or find a job, ask about vocational rehabilitation instead.
  • If there is a waiting list, Time To Be Bold suggests Hadley’s free lessons on living with vision loss, by phone, mail or online: 800-323-4238.

Developmental Disabilities Council for the U.S. Virgin Islands

For: Intellectual or developmental disability

Every state has a governor-appointed Council on Developmental Disabilities, made up mostly of people with developmental disabilities and their family members. It works on policy, advocacy, training and grants rather than direct services, but its staff can point you to the right state agency, local board and self-advocacy group.

Next step: Call the council and ask where to apply for developmental disability services and which self-advocacy groups are near you.

Good to know
  • Policy and advocacy council; it does not run intake or provide direct services.

Rights, advocacy & legal help

Disability rights agencies, complaint offices and legal aid for denials, access barriers, school, health care and voting.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • What happened, where, when and who was involved, written down.
  • Copies of letters, emails, notices and photos.
  • What you asked for and the answer you got.
  • The date on any denial or notice. Deadlines can be short.

Ask

  • Does a law cover this, and which office enforces it?
  • What is my deadline to complain or appeal?
  • Can you represent me, or only advise me?
  • What should I put in writing right now?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Crisis, safety & mental health

Lines to call or text right now, how to report abuse or neglect, and mental health support.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • Where you are right now, in case help needs to find you.
  • For a report about someone else: who is at risk, where they live and what you saw or were told.
  • A safe phone number or email where they can reach you.

Ask

  • What happens after this call?
  • Can I use text, relay, a videophone or an interpreter instead of a voice call?
  • Is there a case or reference number I should keep?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Serves all of U.S. Virgin Islands

Virgin Islands Department of Health: state mental health agency for the U.S. Virgin Islands

For: Mental health condition

Virgin Islands Department of Health

SAMHSA lists this as the state mental health agency for the U.S. Virgin Islands. Each state’s agency oversees mental health treatment programs, and according to SAMHSA it can give more information about treatment services offered in the state and about how treatment facilities are licensed.

Next step: Call or visit the agency’s website and ask how to find public mental health services where you live, such as a community mental health center, and what help there is if you have no insurance or are on Medicaid.

Good to know
  • In a mental health crisis, call or text 988, or chat at 988lifeline.org. You do not need to be suicidal to reach out.
  • This listing comes from SAMHSA’s 2024 national directory; the agency’s name, number or website may have changed since.
