Ramps & home modification

Programs that build, lend or pay for ramps, bathroom changes and other home access work.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • Whether you own or rent. If you rent, ask the landlord in writing before any work.
  • Photos of the entrance and a count of the steps, or the height from the ground to the door.
  • What the person uses to get around: wheelchair, scooter, walker or cane.
  • Household size and income, if the program is based on income.

Ask

  • Does the work have to be approved before it starts?
  • Is this a grant, a loan or a free build by volunteers?
  • Who handles permits and inspections?
  • Is there a waiting list, and how long is it?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Equipment, repair & assistive technology

Try, borrow, repair, finance or reuse wheelchairs and assistive technology.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • The make, model and serial number of the equipment, if you have them.
  • Who paid for it and when: Medicare, Medicaid, the VA, private insurance or you.
  • The name of the supplier or dealer, and any warranty papers.
  • What is wrong, when it started and what it keeps you from doing.

Ask

  • Can I borrow or try equipment while I wait?
  • Is there a cost, and can it be reduced or waived?
  • How long is the wait, and who do I call to check on it?
  • Do you serve my county?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Disability parking placard in Hawaii: how to apply

For: Mobility or physical disability

Disability and Communication Access Board (DCAB), with county issuing offices

In Hawaii, disability parking placards are issued by the Disability and Communication Access Board (DCAB), with county issuing offices. Apply with the Person with a Disability Parking Permit Application (Form PA-3). Who can certify the disability: a licensed physician or advanced practice registered nurse (APRN).

Next step: Open the agency’s page for the current form and have the medical part completed. Take a first-time application and valid ID to your county issuing site: a Satellite City Hall in Honolulu, the Division of Motor Vehicles & Licensing in Maui, the Driver License office in Kauai, or the Office of Aging in Hawaii County. Renew a long-term placard by mail to DCAB, P.O. Box 3377, Honolulu, HI 96801.

Good to know
  • A temporary placard lasts up to six months and costs $12.
  • There is no fee to renew a long-term placard.
  • Forms, fees and rules change. Check the agency’s page before you apply.

Hawaii Department of Transportation: rural public transit program (Section 5311)

Hawaii Department of Transportation

The Hawaii Department of Transportation runs the federal Section 5311 program, which funds public transit in towns and rural areas with fewer than 50,000 people. Rural transit systems in this state are paid for in part through this office, and many offer rides that people with disabilities can use.

Next step: Ask the state transit office, or look on the page, for the public transit provider that serves your county, then call that provider and ask about door-to-door rides and how to register.

Good to know
  • This is a state grants office, not a ride booking line.
  • Rural transit hours, fares and service areas are set by each local provider.

Income, benefits & health coverage

Social Security disability, Medicaid, Medicare counseling, ABLE savings accounts and help appealing a denial.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • Your Social Security number and a photo ID.
  • Names, addresses and dates for your doctors, clinics and hospitals, and a list of your medicines.
  • Recent pay stubs, benefit letters and bank statements.
  • Any denial or decision letter. Note the date on it, because appeal deadlines are counted from the day you receive it.

Ask

  • Which program fits my situation, and can I apply for more than one?
  • What is the deadline to appeal, and where do I send the appeal?
  • Will working or saving money change my benefits?
  • Who can help me fill out the forms at no cost?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

USDA Rural Development home repair loans and grants (Hawaii)

USDA Rural Development, Hilo state office

The USDA Section 504 Home Repair program gives loans to low-income and very-low-income homeowners in eligible rural areas to repair, improve or modernize their homes, and grants to very-low-income homeowners age 62 and older to remove health and safety hazards. Repairs and improvements can sometimes include accessibility changes such as ramps or bathroom work, so ask the local office what qualifies.

Next step: Call the Hawaii Rural Development state office, ask for Single Family Housing, and ask which local office takes 504 repair applications for your county.

Good to know

  • Grants are for homeowners age 62 and older with very low income; funding is limited each federal fiscal year.

Have ready

For: Children, youth and families

Early Intervention Section

The early intervention program in Hawaii serves children from birth to age three who have a developmental delay or disability. Parents do not have to wait for a referral, and the evaluation is free.

Next step: Call the number shown here. Say you are concerned about your child’s development and want an evaluation under Part C of IDEA. Write down the name of each person you speak with and the date.

Vision, hearing & communication

Services for people who are blind, have low vision, are deaf, hard of hearing or DeafBlind: training, reading services, phone equipment and relay.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • A recent eye exam or hearing test, if you have one.
  • What has become hard to do: reading mail, using the phone, getting around, working.
  • Proof of where you live and of income, if the program asks.
  • How you want to be contacted: voice, text, email, videophone or relay.

Ask

  • Do you come to my home, or do I travel to you?
  • Is the equipment free, loaned or sold?
  • Who can sign the form that confirms my vision or hearing loss?
  • Is there training for my family too?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Independent living help for older adults with vision loss in Hawaii

For: Blind or low vision, Older adults

Ho‘opono Services for the Blind, Hawaii Division of Vocational Rehabilitation

This is Hawaii’s program under the federal Independent Living Services for Older Individuals who are Blind (OIB) grant, run by the Ho‘opono Services for the Blind, Hawaii Division of Vocational Rehabilitation. Disability rights agencies, complaint offices and legal aid for denials, access barriers, school, health care and voting.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • What happened, where, when and who was involved, written down.
  • Copies of letters, emails, notices and photos.
  • What you asked for and the answer you got.
  • The date on any denial or notice. Deadlines can be short.

Ask

  • Does a law cover this, and which office enforces it?
  • What is my deadline to complain or appeal?
  • Can you represent me, or only advise me?
  • What should I put in writing right now?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

Statewide

Hawaii State Long-Term Care Ombudsman

Hawaii Executive Office on Aging

Every state has an Office of the State Long-Term Care Ombudsman. Ombudsmen are free, confidential advocates for people living in nursing homes, assisted living and other care facilities. They take complaints about care, rights, discharge and billing and work to resolve them at the resident's direction. Family members, friends and visiting volunteers can call too.

Next step: Call the state ombudsman office and ask to be connected to the local ombudsman for the facility where the resident lives.

Good to know
  • The ombudsman works for the resident and cannot take sides with the facility or family; services are free and confidential.

Statewide

Pacific ADA Center

The Pacific ADA Center is the regional ADA Center for this state in the ADA National Network. It answers questions about the Americans with Disabilities Act, including access to buildings, programs, transportation and employment, and it gives guidance and training. It is hosted by Pacific ADA Center.

Next step: Call or use the website and ask your ADA question; the center can explain the law and your options, but it does not take legal cases.

Good to know
  • ADA Centers give information and guidance; they are not law firms and do not enforce the ADA.
  • The number serves your whole region, not only this state.

Hawaii Adult Protective Services: report abuse, neglect or exploitation

Hawaii Adult Protective Services

The reporting line in Hawaii for suspected abuse, neglect or financial exploitation of older adults and adults with disabilities. Adult Protective Services takes the report, decides whether it meets the state’s rules and looks into it. You can make a report without giving your name.

Next step: Call and say who is at risk, where they are and what you have seen or been told. Ask what happens next and whether you can get an update.

Developmental disability agencies

County boards, regional centers and state offices for developmental disabilities. They decide eligibility, coordinate services and manage waiver funding.

Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • Records that show when the disability began, such as school or medical evaluations.
  • Proof of where you live.
  • A Medicaid card, if you have one.

Ask

  • How do I apply for eligibility, and how long does it take?
  • Is there a waiting list for waiver services?
  • Who will be my service coordinator?
  • What help can I get while I wait?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.
