Caregiver and respite help

For anyone who looks after a family member, friend or veteran with a disability or illness: where to find a break, which public programs help caregivers, and how some families get paid for care. Each statement links to the official page it comes from.

Sources checked November 20, 2026.

In crisis or unsafe right now?

Call 988 or text 988, any time. Deaf or hard of hearing: call 988 from your videophone, text 988, or dial 711 and then 988. In immediate danger, call 911.

Respite care: finding a break

Respite means someone else gives care for a while so you can take a break.

  • Respite is a period of rest or a break from caregiving. It can last a few hours or a few days. [1]
  • Paid respite can happen at home (a worker comes to you), at an adult day program, or as a short stay in a nursing home. [2][1]
  • If your state has a State Respite Coalition or a Lifespan Respite program, ARCH says to contact it first. It can tell you about respite providers and ways to pay for respite. [3]
  • ARCH does not give respite care itself, and a listing in its locator is not an endorsement. ARCH does not check providers’ backgrounds, references or experience, so check any provider before you hire them. [3]

Eldercare Locator and Area Agencies on Aging

One call or one website visit can connect you to caregiver help near you.

  • An Area Agency on Aging is a public or private nonprofit agency that a state names to serve older people in one area: a city, a county or several counties. Local names vary. [4]
  • Through the National Family Caregiver Support Program, the federal government gives grants to states and territories to help family members and other unpaid caregivers. [5]
  • That program pays for five kinds of help: information about services, help getting those services, counseling, support groups and caregiver training, respite care, and some extra services on a limited basis. [5]
  • It serves people caring for someone 60 or older; people caring for someone of any age with Alzheimer’s disease or a related disorder; relatives 55 or older (not parents) raising a child under 18; and relatives 55 or older, parents included, caring for an adult aged 18 to 59 with a disability. [5]
  • The Eldercare Locator connects you to your local and state Area Agencies on Aging and to community groups that support older adults and caregivers. [5][4]

VA help for people caring for a veteran

VA’s Caregiver Support Program has two programs: the Program of General Caregiver Support Services (PGCSS) and the Program of Comprehensive Assistance for Family Caregivers (PCAFC). PCAFC has stricter rules. [7]

VA Caregiver Support Line

U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs

For caregivers, family members, friends, veterans and community partners. Staff share information about support services, give short-term counseling to help with stress and burnout, and can refer you to the Caregiver Support Program team at your local VA.

Next step: Call if you are not sure which VA program fits, or to reach your local VA caregiver team.

Program of General Caregiver Support Services (PGCSS)

VA Caregiver Support Program

For caregivers of veterans of any era who are enrolled in VA health care. The veteran needs help with one or more activities of daily living, or needs supervision or protection because of a neurological or other impairment or injury. You do not have to be related to the veteran or live with them. It offers skills training, online and mobile support, one-to-one coaching, group support, peer support mentoring, and resources and referrals.

Next step: There is no formal application. Contact the Caregiver Support Program team at the veteran’s VA facility, or ask the veteran’s VA provider for a referral. You complete an intake, and the veteran must agree to list you as their caregiver in their health record.

Program of Comprehensive Assistance for Family Caregivers (PCAFC)

VA Caregiver Support Program

For family caregivers of veterans who had a serious injury or illness incurred or made worse in the line of duty, in any era. The veteran must have a VA disability rating of 70% or higher, be discharged (or have a date of medical discharge), be enrolled in VA health care, and need at least 6 months of continuous, in-person personal care. The caregiver must be at least 18 and be a family member, or live full time with the veteran (or be willing to). A veteran can name 1 primary and up to 2 secondary caregivers.

All approved caregivers can get education and training, mental health counseling, and certain travel benefits. Primary caregivers may also get a monthly stipend, CHAMPVA health coverage if they have no other health plan, legal and financial planning help, and at least 30 days of respite care a year.

Next step: The veteran and caregiver apply together on VA Form 10-10CG: online, by mail, or in person with the caregiver support team at the nearest VA medical center. VA says not to send medical records with the application.

VA respite care for veterans

VA Geriatrics and Extended Care

VA pays for care for a short time when a family caregiver needs a break: a worker comes to the home, the veteran goes to an adult day health care program, or the veteran has a short stay in a VA Community Living Center or a community nursing home. All enrolled veterans are eligible if they meet the clinical criteria and the service is available. A copay may be charged, based on service-connected disability status and financial information.

Next step: Ask the veteran’s VA social worker or case manager to help complete the Application for Extended Care Benefits (VA Form 10-10EC).

Medicaid home and community-based services

Medicaid can pay for care at home in many states. Each state runs its own programs, so the answer comes from your state.

  • Medicaid home and community-based services (HCBS) waivers serve people who want long-term care at home or in the community instead of in an institution. [14]
  • Forty-seven states and D.C. run at least one of these waivers, called 1915(c) waivers. [15]
  • Each state chooses who its waivers serve. Medicaid.gov names older adults, children who depend on medical technology, people with behavioral conditions and people with intellectual disabilities. A waiver can also be aimed at an age group or a diagnosis, such as autism, epilepsy, cerebral palsy, traumatic brain injury or HIV/AIDS. [14]
  • Services a waiver can cover include personal care, adult day health services, habilitation and respite care. [14]
  • Each state chooses the most people a waiver will serve, so ask whether there is a wait. [14]
  • To learn what your state offers and how to apply, contact your state Medicaid office. [16]

Your state’s Medicaid agency is listed on your state’s page and under Benefits and health coverage in the directory.

Getting paid to care for a family member

Some programs pay a family member or friend to give care. Who can be paid, and how much, depends on the program and the state.

  • Medicaid: if the person already gets Medicaid, their state may let a family member or friend become a paid caregiver, often through a consumer-directed personal assistance program. Requirements and pay vary by state. [16]
  • In Medicaid self-directed services, the person getting care (or their representative) can have the authority to recruit, hire, train and supervise the people who give their care. [17]
  • Under one Medicaid option for self-directed personal assistance, a state may choose to let people hire legally liable relatives, such as parents or spouses. [18]
  • VA: primary caregivers in the comprehensive caregiver program (PCAFC) may get a monthly stipend. See the VA section above. [9]
  • VA: Veteran-Directed Care gives a veteran a budget for home and community-based services. With a counselor, the veteran hires their own workers, which might include a family member or neighbor. Enrolled veterans can get it if they are eligible for community care, meet the clinical criteria and it is offered where they live. Ask the veteran’s VA social worker. [19]
  • VA: Aid and Attendance adds monthly payments to a VA pension for veterans and survivors who qualify, which can help pay for a caregiver. [16]
  • Long-term care insurance: some policies let family members be paid as caregivers. Ask the insurance agent to confirm the benefits in writing. [16]
  • Paid family leave: some states have programs that pay you to take time off work to care for a family member. Ask your state labor office. [16]

Taking care of yourself

Caregivers need support too. These are free places to start.

  • VA describes three kinds of respite: self-guided, informal and formal. Formal respite is short-term paid help from a professional. [1]
  • Caregivers of veterans can call the VA Caregiver Support Line for short-term counseling to help with stress and burnout and to learn coping skills. [8]
  • PGCSS offers peer support mentoring, group support and one-to-one coaching for caregivers of veterans. [10]
  • The National Family Caregiver Support Program pays for counseling, support groups and caregiver training. The Eldercare Locator can help you find them near you. [5]
  • If you are a veteran, or you care for one, and you are in crisis: call 988 and press 1, or text 838255, to reach the Veterans Crisis Line. [1]
