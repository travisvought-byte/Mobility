Service animals: your rights and where to get a trained dog

Have a service dog, or hoping to get one? Here is what staff may and may not ask, where your dog can go, your rights in rented housing and on planes, how to complain and where to find a trained dog. Each statement links to the official source it comes from.

Rules differ by place. The ADA covers businesses and state and local governments. The Fair Housing Act covers housing. The Department of Transportation’s rules cover flights. Your state may give more protection.

Deaf, hard of hearing or have a speech disability? Dial 711 to place a free relay call to any phone number listed here.

What counts as a service animal

  • Under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA), a service animal is a dog that is individually trained to do work or perform tasks for a person with a disability. [1]
  • The task must be directly related to the person’s disability. Examples the Justice Department gives: guiding people who are blind, alerting people who are deaf and pulling a wheelchair. [1]
  • A dog whose only job is to give comfort or emotional support does not qualify under the ADA. [1]
  • A dog trained to sense that an anxiety attack is about to happen, and to act to help avoid it or lessen it, does qualify. A dog whose mere presence gives comfort does not. [2]
  • The dog does not have to come from a professional training program. People with disabilities have the right to train the dog themselves. [2]
  • Under the ADA, the dog must already be trained before it goes into public places. A dog still in training is not a service animal under the ADA. [2]
  • Any breed can be a service animal. A dog may not be kept out because of assumptions or stereotypes about its breed. [2]
  • Miniature horses have their own rule. Businesses and state and local governments must make reasonable changes to allow a miniature horse that is individually trained to do work or perform tasks for a person with a disability. The Justice Department says these horses are generally 24 to 34 inches tall at the shoulder and weigh 70 to 100 pounds. [1][3][4]
  • To decide whether a miniature horse can come into a particular place, they look at whether it is housebroken, whether it is under the handler’s control, whether the place can handle its type, size and weight, and whether its presence would compromise legitimate safety requirements. [1][3]

The two questions staff may ask

When it is not obvious what the dog does, staff may ask only two questions:

  1. Is the dog a service animal required because of a disability?
  2. What work or task has the dog been trained to perform?

Sources: [1][3][4]

  • Staff generally do not ask these questions when it is readily apparent what the dog does. [3][4]
  • Staff may not ask about your disability or ask for medical documentation. [1][3]
  • Staff may not require a special ID card or training documentation for the dog, and may not ask the dog to show it can do its task. [1][2]

Where service animals can go

  • The ADA rule covers businesses open to the public (Title III) and state and local government services (Title II). [3][4]
  • A person with a disability may bring a service animal into all areas where members of the public are allowed to go. [1][3][4]
  • Allergies and fear of dogs are not valid reasons to deny access or refuse service. Where possible, both people are accommodated, for example in different parts of the room or in different rooms. [1]
  • Restaurants and stores: service animals must be allowed to go with their handlers to and through self-service food lines. [2]
  • Hotels: a guest with a service animal may not be limited to “pet-friendly” rooms, and the hotel may not charge for cleaning the hair or dander a service animal sheds. [2]
  • Hospitals: service animals must generally be allowed in patient rooms and other places patients and the public may go. A hospital may keep a dog out of places like operating rooms or burn units, where it could compromise a sterile environment. [1][2]
  • Places of worship: religious institutions and organizations are specifically exempt from the ADA. State laws may still apply. [2]
  • Housing and air travel follow other laws, explained below. Federal agencies are covered by Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act, not the ADA. [2]

What businesses may not require

  • No vest, ID tag or special harness. The ADA does not require any of them. [2]
  • No certificate or registration to get in. The Justice Department does not recognize online certificates or registrations as proof that a dog is a service animal. [2]
  • No extra fee or deposit, even where pet owners must pay one. A business may charge for damage the dog causes only if it normally charges other customers for damage they cause. [1][3][4]
  • No separate seating or worse treatment. People with service animals may not be isolated from other customers, treated less favorably, or charged fees others do not pay. [1]
  • The business is not responsible for caring for or watching the dog. That is the handler’s job. [3][4]
  • Local rules still apply. Service dogs must follow local vaccination rules and local dog licensing and registration rules that apply to all dogs. A city or county may not require service animals to be registered as service animals; voluntary registries are allowed. [2]

When an animal can be asked to leave

  • Staff may ask that a service animal be removed only if the dog is out of control and the handler does not take effective action to control it, or if the dog is not housebroken. [1][3][4]
  • Barking once, or barking because someone provoked it, does not mean the dog is out of control. [2]
  • If the dog is properly asked to leave, the person must still be offered the goods or services without the dog there. [1][3][4]
  • The dog must be under the handler’s control. It must be harnessed, leashed or tethered unless the person’s disability prevents that or the device would interfere with the dog’s work. Then the handler keeps control by voice, signals or other effective means. [1][3][4]

Emotional support animals and service animals

  • Under the ADA, emotional support, therapy, comfort and companion animals are not service animals. [1][2]
  • Housing is different. The ADA definition does not limit the broader definition of “assistance animal” under the Fair Housing Act. See the housing section below. [1]
  • Air travel: emotional support animals are not service animals, and an airline may treat one as a pet. [5][6]
  • Some state and local laws define service animal more broadly than the ADA does. The Justice Department suggests checking with your state attorney general’s office. [1]

Housing: the Fair Housing Act

  • The Fair Housing Act applies to virtually all types of housing, public and privately owned. [2]
  • A housing provider must make reasonable accommodations, which are changes or exceptions to a rule or policy that a person with a disability needs to have an equal chance to use and enjoy their home. HUD and the Justice Department give the example of a deaf tenant whose dog alerts them to sounds: the provider must make an exception to its “no pets” policy. [9]
  • If the disability or the need for the animal is not obvious, the provider may ask for reliable information that shows the disability and how the animal relates to it. A provider may not usually ask about the nature or severity of the disability, and medical records are usually not needed. [9]
  • A housing provider may not charge extra fees or deposits as a condition of the accommodation. It may charge for damage the animal causes, or take it from the usual security deposit, if it normally charges tenants for damage. [9]
  • Know this: on September 17, 2025, HUD withdrew its two notices on assistance animals in housing (from 2013 and 2020) and said they should not be relied on while HUD reviews them. The 2004 joint statement used above was not on that list. Ask HUD or a local fair housing center how the rules apply to you. [7][9]

Air travel: Department of Transportation rules

  • For flights, a service animal is a dog, of any breed or type, that is individually trained to do work or perform tasks for a person with a disability, including a psychiatric, intellectual or other mental disability. Airlines must treat psychiatric service animals the same as other service animals. [5][8]
  • Staff may ask whether the dog is needed because of a disability and what work or task it is trained to do. They may not ask about the nature of your disability or ask the dog to show its task. [10]
  • Airlines may require the U.S. DOT Service Animal Air Transportation Form. For flights scheduled for 8 hours or more, they may also require the DOT relief attestation form. They may not require other documents, except to meet other government animal rules. [11][5]
  • If you booked more than 48 hours before departure, the airline may ask for the form up to 48 hours ahead. If you booked later than that, it may not require advance notice, but may ask for the form at the gate. [11]
  • An airline does not have to accept more than two service animals for one passenger. [12]
  • The dog rides on your lap or in your foot space, if that is safe and does not crowd another passenger. If it does not fit, the airline must offer another seat in the same class if one is open. If nothing works, it must offer to carry the dog in the cargo hold free of charge, or let you take a later flight with room. [13]
  • Airlines may require the dog to be harnessed, leashed or tethered at all times in the airport areas they control and on the plane. [10]
  • An airline may refuse a service animal that is a direct threat to others’ health or safety, causes a significant disruption in the cabin or gate area, would break federal safety or health rules, or when the required forms are not provided. It must judge the individual dog, not its breed, and must not refuse if a lesser step, such as a muzzle for a barking dog, would solve the problem. [14]
  • Airlines may not require a passenger with a service animal to check in in person at the airport instead of online. [8]
  • Airlines may not refuse a service animal just because it makes other passengers or crew uncomfortable. [5]

How to file a complaint

Write down what happened, where and when, who you spoke with and what they said. Keep copies.

A business or a state or local government

U.S. Department of Justice, Civil Rights Division (ADA)

  1. Report it online on the Justice Department’s civil rights portal, or mail the ADA complaint form or a letter to: U.S. Department of Justice, Civil Rights Division, 950 Pennsylvania Avenue, NW, Washington, DC 20530.
  2. Call the ADA Information Line if you are not sure the ADA covers what happened. ADA.gov suggests calling if you have not heard back after three months.
  3. You may also file a private lawsuit in federal court.

Housing

U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development, Office of Fair Housing and Equal Opportunity (FHEO)

  1. Report it online, or talk with an FHEO intake specialist by phone. You can also print the complaint form and mail it to your regional FHEO office.
  2. There are time limits on filing with HUD, so report it as soon as possible.

HUD discrimination line. TTY 1-800-927-9275. HUD also takes calls through relay services.

Air travel

The airline first, then the U.S. Department of Transportation

  1. At the airport, ask for the airline’s Complaints Resolution Official (CRO). Airlines must make one available at no cost, in person or by phone.
  2. For a problem that needs help right away, call the DOT hotline for air travelers with disabilities, weekdays 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. Eastern, except federal holidays. If you are deaf, hard of hearing or have a speech disability, dial 711 for relay.
  3. To have DOT look into it, file a complaint online or by mail: Office of Aviation Consumer Protection, U.S. Department of Transportation, 1200 New Jersey Avenue, SE, Washington, DC 20590.

Where to get a trained service dog

Getting a trained dog takes time. Ask each program about its waiting list, costs, who it serves and what it expects of you. This list is not complete, and being listed here is not an endorsement.

VA service dog benefit for veterans

U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs

VA does not provide service dogs. For veterans diagnosed with a visual, hearing or substantial mobility impairment, VA can pay for veterinary care for one service dog at a time through an insurance policy, plus certain equipment and travel costs. A mental health team can also decide whether a mental health condition is the main cause of a veteran’s substantial mobility limits. The dog and the veteran must finish training with an organization accredited by Assistance Dogs International or the International Guide Dog Federation. The veteran pays for things like food, grooming and license tags.

Next step: Talk with your VA health care provider, or your VA mental health provider, to start the application.

Quick answers

What questions can a store or restaurant ask about my service dog?

When it is not obvious what the dog does, staff may ask only two questions: is the dog a service animal required because of a disability, and what work or task has it been trained to perform. They may not ask about your disability, ask for medical or training papers, or ask the dog to show its task. [1][3]

Does my service dog need a vest, ID or certificate?

No. The ADA does not require a vest, ID tag or special harness, and the Justice Department does not recognize online certificates or registrations as proof that a dog is a service animal. [2]

Can a business charge a fee or deposit for my service dog?

No. A business may not charge a fee or deposit for a service animal, even if it charges pet owners. It may charge for damage the dog causes if it normally charges other customers for damage. [1][3]

When can a business ask me to remove my service dog?

Only if the dog is out of control and you do not take effective action to control it, or if the dog is not housebroken. If that happens, the business must still offer you its goods or services without the dog there. [3][1]

Is an emotional support animal a service animal?

Not under the ADA, and not for air travel. Housing is different: the Fair Housing Act can require a landlord to make an exception to a no-pets rule for an animal a person with a disability needs. [1][5][9]

Can a miniature horse be a service animal?

The ADA has a separate rule for miniature horses. Businesses and state and local governments must make reasonable changes to allow a trained miniature horse, depending on whether it is housebroken and under control, whether the place can handle its size and weight, and safety. [3][1]

Where do I file a complaint about a service animal being refused?

For a business or a state or local government, file with the U.S. Department of Justice. For housing, report it to HUD. For air travel, ask the airline’s Complaints Resolution Official first, then file with the U.S. Department of Transportation. [15][16][5]
