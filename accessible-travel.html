Accessible travel: flying, trains, buses and parks

Planning a trip with a wheelchair or another disability? Here is what airlines must do for you, how to get help at airport security, how to book accessible space on Amtrak and intercity buses, and how to get the free Access Pass for national parks. Flying with a wheelchair or other disability

The Air Carrier Access Act rule applies to all flights of U.S. airlines, and to flights to or from the United States by foreign airlines. [1]

  • Airlines must help you get from the curb to your departing flight, and between gates to make a connection. [2]
  • An airline cannot leave you unattended for more than 30 minutes in a wheelchair or other device you cannot move in on your own. [2]
  • Airlines must check your wheelchair or other assistive device at the gate and return it to you at the gate on time, so you can use it in the terminal. [2]
  • Collapsible wheelchairs and other assistive devices have priority for space in the cabin, and assistive devices do not count against the limit on carry-on bags. [1]
  • If an airline loses, damages or destroys your wheelchair or other assistive device, it must pay compensation up to the device’s original purchase price. [2]
  • Most aircraft with 19 or more seats must have a ramp or mechanical lift for boarding at airports with more than 10,000 boardings a year. [1]
  • Airlines may not keep anyone out of a specific seat because of a disability, unless an FAA or foreign government safety rule requires it, as it can for exit rows. [1]
  • New aircraft with 30 or more seats must have movable armrests on half the aisle seats, and new aircraft with 100 or more seats must have priority space in the cabin for a folding wheelchair. [1]
  • Airlines generally cannot require advance notice, but may ask for up to 48 hours’ notice for certain help, such as hooking up a respirator to the aircraft’s power or carrying an electric wheelchair on an aircraft with fewer than 60 seats. [1]

Airport security: TSA Cares and screening

TSA Cares can arrange for a Passenger Support Specialist, a TSA officer with special training in helping and communicating with travelers who have disabilities or medical conditions. [4]

Before and at the checkpoint

  1. Contact TSA Cares at least 72 hours before your flight, by phone or with its online request form. [4]
  2. At the checkpoint, ask a TSA officer or supervisor for a Passenger Support Specialist if you did not arrange one ahead. [4]
  3. Before screening starts, tell the officer whether you can walk or stand on your own. [5]
  • Asking for a Passenger Support Specialist does not exempt you from security screening. [4]
  • You can be screened while seated in your wheelchair or scooter. If you cannot be screened by technology, you get a pat-down. [5]
  • Officers screen the wheelchair or scooter too, including its seat cushions. Items are tested for traces of explosives, and removable items go through the X-ray. [5]
  • Travelers 75 or older in a wheelchair or scooter may remain seated. In TSA PreCheck lanes, travelers who cannot stand or walk may remain seated in their mobility device. [5]

Amtrak trains

Spaces for wheelchairs and other wheeled mobility devices, and seats you can transfer into, are limited, so reserve ahead. [6]

  • You can reserve accessible space on Amtrak.com or the Amtrak app, by phone, or at a staffed station during ticket office hours. Between 7 a.m. and 11 p.m. Eastern, you can also text Amtrak’s reservation number or use web chat. [6]
  • A group that needs wheelchair seating together should ask Amtrak group sales at least 14 days ahead. [6]
  • Adults with a disability get 10% off the rail fare. A child with a disability gets the 50% child discount plus another 10% off, and one companion who can give needed help also gets 10% off. [7]
  • On the Downeaster, between Boston and Portland, Maine, the disability discount is 50%. Riders on the Downeaster, Pennsylvanian and Keystone must show documentation of their disability. [7]
  • These discounts may not combine with other discount programs. [7]

Intercity buses

Federal ADA rules set what companies running over-the-road buses, the coach buses used between cities, must do for passengers with disabilities. [8]

  • Large bus companies had to make 100 percent of the buses they use for fixed-route service accessible by October 29, 2012. [8]
  • Until a company’s fixed-route fleet is fully accessible, it must give you an accessible bus when you ask, but it may require up to 48 hours’ notice. [8]
  • For charter and other demand-responsive trips, a company may ask for up to 48 hours’ notice to provide an accessible bus. With less notice, it must still provide one if it can with a reasonable effort. [8]
  • Companies must help passengers with disabilities get on and off as needed, including at rest stops and stops along the way. [8]
  • A company may not use or ask people who are not its employees, such as other passengers, to give routine help, unless you ask for or agree to their help. [8]

Rental cars and hand controls

Federal ADA rules for businesses list installing vehicle hand controls as an example of removing a barrier, which a business must do where it is readily achievable: easy to do without much difficulty or expense. [10]

  • The same list of examples includes installing ramps, making curb cuts in sidewalks and entrances, and installing grab bars in toilet stalls. [10]

Ask for hand controls when you reserve, and confirm with the rental location before you travel. If you need a vehicle of your own, Help paying for an accessible vehicle covers grants, rebates and loans.

National parks and federal lands: the Access Pass

The Access Pass is free for U.S. citizens and residents of any age with a permanent disability. [11]

  • The disability does not have to be 100%, but it must severely limit one or more major life activities. [11]
  • It is one of the America the Beautiful passes, which cover entrance fees and standard amenity (day-use) fees at lands run by the National Park Service, U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service, U.S. Forest Service, Bureau of Land Management, Bureau of Reclamation and U.S. Army Corps of Engineers. [12]
  • Bring a photo ID and one proof of disability: a signed letter from a licensed physician, a document from a federal agency such as VA or Social Security showing you get disability benefits, or a document from a state agency such as vocational rehabilitation. [11]
  • Getting the pass in person, at more than 1,000 federal recreation sites, is recommended. A digital pass from Recreation.gov can be used right away. [11]
  • A pass ordered by mail from the USGS Online Store can take up to three weeks to arrive, and the store lists the Access Pass at $10.00. [11][13]

If something goes wrong

Raise a problem with the airline while you are still at the airport if you can, then put it in writing.

A problem with a flight, in order

  1. At the airport, ask for the airline’s complaints resolution official (CRO). Airlines must have one available at each airport they serve. [1][2]
  2. For a time-sensitive problem, call the DOT disability hotline. [3]
  3. Send the airline a written complaint. It must answer the disability issues in writing within 30 days, but it does not have to answer a complaint sent more than 45 days after the incident unless DOT refers it. [2]
  4. To have DOT investigate, send DOT your complaint in writing or by email. DOT refers disability complaints it receives within 6 months of the incident to the airline. [3][2]
  • Problems with a bus company, such as a broken wheelchair lift or a driver not offering required help, can be reported to FMCSA online or by phone. [9]

Know your rights lists who handles each kind of problem, from air travel to public transit.

Quick answers

What are my rights when flying with a wheelchair?

Airlines must help you from the curb to your flight and between gates, and cannot leave you unattended for more than 30 minutes in a device you cannot move on your own. Collapsible wheelchairs have priority for cabin space, assistive devices do not count against carry-on limits, and an airline that loses or damages your wheelchair must pay up to its original purchase price. [2][1]

How do I get help through airport security?

Contact TSA Cares at (855) 787-2227 at least 72 hours before your flight, or ask a TSA officer or supervisor at the checkpoint for a Passenger Support Specialist. It does not exempt you from screening. [4]

Can I stay in my wheelchair during TSA screening?

Yes. You can be screened while seated in your wheelchair or scooter, and the chair is screened too. If you cannot be screened by technology, you get a pat-down. [5]

Does Amtrak have a disability discount?

Adults with a disability get 10% off the rail fare, and one companion who can give needed help also gets 10% off. A child with a disability gets the 50% child discount plus another 10%. On the Downeaster the discount is 50%. [7]

Is the national parks Access Pass free?

Yes, for U.S. citizens and residents of any age with a permanent disability. Get it in person at a federal recreation site or as a digital pass from Recreation.gov. For a pass ordered by mail, the USGS Online Store lists a $10.00 price. [11][13]

What can I do if an airline damages my wheelchair?

Ask for the airline’s complaints resolution official at the airport. The airline must pay compensation up to the wheelchair’s original purchase price, and must answer a written complaint within 30 days. To have DOT investigate, send DOT a written or email complaint. [1][2][3]
