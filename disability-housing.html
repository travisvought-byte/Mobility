Housing Choice Vouchers (Section 8) and disability

A housing choice voucher pays all or part of the rent on a home you choose, such as an apartment, townhouse or single-family home. Local public housing agencies run the program and decide who qualifies. [1]

How to apply for a voucher

  1. Find your local public housing agency (PHA). It decides whether you are eligible and takes applications. [1]
  2. Ask whether its waiting list is open. Lists often close when they hold more families than the PHA can help, so check back to see when yours reopens. [1]
  3. Ask about its preferences. A PHA may give a preference to families that include a person with a disability, but not to people with one specific disability. [2]
  4. When you get a voucher, look for a home that meets the program’s rules. If your disability makes the search take longer, ask for more time as a reasonable accommodation. [1][3]
  • Eligibility is based on your total annual gross income, your family size and your citizenship or immigration status. [1]
  • To qualify, a family generally must be very low income under the limits HUD sets for each area. At least 75 percent of the families a PHA admits from its waiting list each year must be extremely low income. [4][1]
  • What you pay is generally the highest of 30 percent of your monthly adjusted income, 10 percent of your monthly income, or the PHA’s minimum rent. The PHA pays its part of the rent to the landlord. [5][1]
  • A family whose head, co-head, spouse or only member has a disability is a disabled family. It gets a set deduction from income that HUD adjusts each year. [6][7]
  • An elderly or disabled family can also deduct unreimbursed health and medical care costs. Any family can deduct attendant care and equipment costs for a member with a disability when they make it possible for someone in the family to work. These deductions count only for the part above 10 percent of annual income. [7]
  • SSI and Social Security disability benefits count as income, because HUD counts everything a family receives unless its rules leave it out. Back payments of SSI or Social Security, whether paid in a lump sum or in later monthly amounts, do not count, and neither does money SSI ignores because it is set aside under a Plan to Achieve Self-Support (PASS). [8]
  • Payments from a state Medicaid agency or another state or federal agency that make it possible for a family member with a disability to live in the home do not count as income. That includes pay a family member gets through such an agency for the care that lets that person live at home. [8]
  • If a PHA prefers working families, it must give that preference to applicants whose head and spouse, or only member, has a disability or is 62 or older, even if they do not work. [2]
  • A voucher’s first search term is at least 60 days. The PHA must extend it as long as reasonably needed when a family member with a disability needs the extra time. [3]
  • As a reasonable accommodation, a PHA may raise the payment standard for your family to up to 120 percent of the area’s fair market rent without HUD’s approval, and higher with HUD’s approval. [9]
  • A live-in aide approved by the PHA to care for a family member with a disability counts when the PHA decides how many bedrooms you get. The PHA may grant an exception to its bedroom rules because of a family member’s health or disability. [10]

Mainstream and Non-Elderly Disabled (NED) vouchers

Some housing choice vouchers are set aside for people with disabilities who are under 62. They work like other vouchers, so ask your public housing agency whether it has them. [12][11]

  • Mainstream vouchers serve any family that includes a person with a disability who is at least 18 and not yet 62 when assistance starts. [12]
  • Once a family is helped with a Mainstream voucher, it does not lose it when that person turns 62. [12]
  • Mainstream vouchers are regular housing choice vouchers with extra eligibility rules, and the same regulations apply to them. [12]
  • A PHA may not keep a separate waiting list for Mainstream vouchers. It gives them to the first eligible family on its regular voucher list. [12]
  • A PHA may adopt a preference for people moving out of institutions or other segregated settings, people at serious risk of entering one, and people who are homeless or at risk of homelessness. [12]
  • For a Non-Elderly Disabled (NED) voucher you must be the head, spouse or co-head of a family, be under 62, have a disability, and meet the income and other rules where you live. [11]

Section 811 supportive housing

HUD’s Section 811 program helps create rental housing for people with disabilities who have very low incomes, with supportive services available to the people who live there. [13][14]

  • Section 811 supports the creation of multifamily housing for very low-income persons with disabilities. [13]
  • Through Section 811 Project Rental Assistance, HUD funds state housing agencies to help pay rent in units for extremely low-income persons with disabilities. [14]
  • Project Rental Assistance provides permanent housing with supportive services available, through partnerships between state housing agencies and state health and human services or Medicaid agencies. [14]
  • For more about Section 811 housing near you, HUD says to contact your local HUD Multifamily office. [13]

Public housing and reasonable accommodations

Public housing offers affordable rental homes to people with low incomes, seniors and people with disabilities. Because it is federally assisted, the housing provider pays for the reasonable accommodations a resident or applicant with a disability needs. [15][16]

  • Your local PHA decides whether you qualify, based on your annual gross income, whether you are a senior, a person with a disability or a family, and your citizenship or immigration status. You may need to apply in person. [15]
  • A reasonable accommodation is a change, exception or adjustment to a rule, policy, practice or service, such as an exception to a parking rule, an interpreter, or papers in large print. [16]
  • A reasonable modification is a structural change to the building or unit, such as a ramp into a building. [16]
  • In federally assisted housing, the provider pays for reasonable accommodations and cannot make an applicant or tenant pay for them. [16]
  • You can ask for an accommodation or modification in writing, but you do not have to. [16]
  • In a new federally assisted apartment project, at least 5 percent of the units, and never fewer than one, must be accessible for people with mobility disabilities, plus another 2 percent for people with hearing or vision disabilities. [17]
  • An empty accessible unit must be offered first to a current resident who needs its features, then to a qualified applicant on the waiting list who needs them. A tenant without a disability who takes one may have to agree to move when another unit opens. [18]
  • If your request is denied, you can file a complaint with HUD or with a local Fair Housing Assistance Program agency. File as soon as you can, because there are time limits. [16]

Finding an affordable or accessible unit

In subsidized rental housing, the government pays apartment owners to lower the rent for tenants with low incomes. Each property takes its own applications, and some keep waiting lists. [19]

How to search HUD’s map

  1. Open HUD’s resource map and choose “Find Affordable Housing Opportunities Near Me.” [19]
  2. Search for your city or address, and look through the affordable housing in your area. [19]
  3. Contact each property you are interested in to ask how to apply for a low-rent unit. [19]
  • Each city or county has its own eligibility rules for affordable housing. [19]
  • Under the Fair Housing Act rules, covered apartment buildings first occupied after March 13, 1991 must have an accessible entrance on an accessible route, doors wide enough for a wheelchair, light switches and outlets in accessible places, bathroom walls strong enough for grab bars, and kitchens and bathrooms a wheelchair user can use. [20]
  • Ask the property manager which units have accessibility features, and ask for a reasonable accommodation if you need one to apply or to live there. [16]

Rural rentals through USDA

USDA Rural Development finances rental housing in rural areas for people with very low to moderate incomes, people 62 or older and people with disabilities. In some of these properties, USDA Rental Assistance lowers the rent. [21][22]

  • Rental Assistance pays owners of USDA-financed rental housing on behalf of tenants with low incomes who cannot pay their full rent. [22]
  • Tenants with very low incomes, below 50 percent of the area median, get first priority. Low income is 50 to 80 percent of the area median. [22]
  • Tenants who receive Rental Assistance pay 30 percent of their income in rent. [23]
  • To apply, contact the management agency listed for the property. Each listing links to the property’s income limits and to the USDA office that serves it. [24]

Fair housing rights and filing a HUD complaint

Three federal laws require housing providers to make reasonable accommodations and modifications for people with disabilities: the Fair Housing Act, Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act and the Americans with Disabilities Act. [16]

File a fair housing complaint with HUD

U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development, Office of Fair Housing and Equal Opportunity

File online, by phone, or by mailing a form or a letter, if a landlord, seller or housing provider refused a reasonable accommodation or modification or treated you unfairly because of a disability.

  • In private housing, a landlord must let you make a reasonable modification under the Fair Housing Act but can require you to pay for it. [16]
  • A letter to HUD should give your name and address, the name and address of the person you are complaining about, the address of the home, the date it happened and a short description of what happened. [25]
  • You must tell HUD within one year. If the discrimination happened more than once or is still going on, HUD must hear from you within one year of the last incident. [26]

Help paying energy bills and other costs

The Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program (LIHEAP) helps pay heating and cooling bills, and the Weatherization Assistance Program helps make homes cheaper to heat and cool. Each state runs its own program and sets its own rules. [27]

  • LIHEAP can help pay heating or cooling bills, and gives emergency help during an energy crisis. Depending on where you live, it may also help with electric bills. [27]
  • To apply, find your state or territory’s LIHEAP office. Some take applications online, and others ask you to apply in person. [27]
  • Federal law lets states give LIHEAP to households where someone receives SSI, and to households with incomes up to 150 percent of the poverty level or 60 percent of the state median income, whichever is higher. [28]
  • States must reach out especially to eligible households with older or disabled members and households with high energy bills. [28]
  • Having a disability may affect whether a utility can shut off your service, depending on your state’s rules. [27]
  • For local help paying other bills or finding food, dial 211. Every call is confidential. [29]

Quick answers

Can I get Section 8 if I am disabled?

A disability does not qualify you by itself. Your public housing agency looks at your income, family size and immigration status. Agencies may give a preference to families that include a person with a disability, and some vouchers, such as Mainstream and NED vouchers, are set aside for people with disabilities under 62. [1][2][12][11]

What are Mainstream vouchers?

Housing choice vouchers for families that include a person with a disability who is at least 18 and not yet 62 when assistance starts. They follow the same rules as other vouchers, come from the agency’s regular waiting list, and families do not lose them when that person turns 62. [12]

What is Section 811 housing?

Rental housing for very low-income people with disabilities, created with HUD funds. Through Project Rental Assistance, state housing agencies help pay rent in units for extremely low-income people with disabilities, with supportive services available. [13][14]

How much rent will I pay with a housing voucher?

Generally the highest of 30 percent of your monthly adjusted income, 10 percent of your monthly income, or the agency’s minimum rent. A disabled family gets a set deduction from income, and can deduct health and medical care costs above 10 percent of annual income. [5][7]

Does SSI or SSDI count as income for Section 8?

Yes. HUD counts SSI and Social Security disability benefits as income. Back payments paid in a lump sum or in later monthly amounts do not count, and neither does money set aside under an SSI Plan to Achieve Self-Support. A disabled family also gets a set deduction from income. [8][7]

Does my landlord have to pay for a ramp?

In federally assisted housing, such as public housing, the provider pays for reasonable accommodations and cannot make you pay. In private housing, the landlord must let you make a reasonable modification but can require you to pay for it. [16]

How do I file a housing discrimination complaint with HUD?

Use HUD’s online form, call 1-800-669-9777, or mail a form or a letter. You must tell HUD within one year, or within one year of the last incident if it happened more than once or is still going on. [25][26]

Can I get help paying my electric or heating bill?

LIHEAP helps pay heating and cooling bills, and in some places electric bills. Each state sets its own rules; find your state or territory’s LIHEAP office to apply. [27]
