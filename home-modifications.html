Help paying for home modifications

Need a ramp, a stairlift, grab bars or wider doors at home? Here is who helps pay: VA grants and HISA, USDA repair loans and grants, Medicaid waivers, Area Agencies on Aging, state assistive technology programs, and the tax rules if you pay yourself. VA: HISA and the SAH, SHA and TRA grants

VA has a smaller lifetime benefit for medically necessary changes to a veteran’s home, such as permanent ramps and roll-in showers, and larger grants to buy, build or change a home for veterans and service members with certain service-connected disabilities. [1][2]

Home Improvements and Structural Alterations (HISA)

VA Prosthetic and Sensory Aids Service

Provides medically necessary improvements and structural alterations to a veteran’s or service member’s primary home, such as permanent ramps and roll-in showers.

Lifetime benefit: $6,800 for changes for a service-connected disability, or for any disability if the veteran has a service-connected disability rated at least 50 percent; $2,000 for a disability not covered by those rules.

Next step: Ask the veteran’s VA care team about a prescription from a VA physician. The application package includes VA Form 10-0103.

Specially Adapted Housing (SAH) and Special Home Adaptation (SHA) grants

U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs

Grants for veterans and service members with certain service-connected disabilities who are using the money to buy, build or change their permanent home.

For fiscal year 2027, an SAH grant pays up to $133,042 and an SHA grant up to $26,654. SAH money can be used up to 6 different times over your lifetime.

Next step: Apply online, by mail or in person.

Sources: [2]

  • All HISA projects must be medically justified, and VA may inspect the site. [1]
  • SAH covers conditions such as ALS, the loss or loss of use of more than one limb, blindness in both eyes (20/200 visual acuity or less) and certain severe burns. You must own or be going to own the home. [2]
  • SHA covers conditions such as the loss or loss of use of both hands, certain severe burns and certain respiratory or breathing injuries. You or a family member must own or be going to own the home. [2]
  • If you are living for now in a family member’s home that needs changes, a Temporary Residence Adaptation (TRA) grant pays up to $53,585 if you qualify for SAH, or up to $9,568 if you qualify for SHA, for fiscal year 2027. [2]

USDA home repair loans and grants (Section 504)

USDA’s Section 504 Home Repair program lends to low- and very-low-income homeowners, and gives grants to very-low-income homeowners 62 or older, to make a home accessible for a household member with a disability or to remove health and safety hazards. [3]

  • The maximum loan is $40,000 and the maximum grant is $10,000. A loan and a grant can be combined for up to $50,000. [3]
  • Loans have a fixed 1 percent interest rate and are repaid over 20 years. [3]
  • Grants can be used only to remove health and safety hazards or make a home accessible for a household member with a disability. Loans can also repair, improve or modernize a home. [3]
  • Grants have a lifetime limit of $10,000, and must be repaid if the home is sold in less than three years. [3]
  • To apply you must own and live in the home, be unable to get affordable credit elsewhere, and have household income under the county limit. Grants also need you to be 62 or older with very-low income. The home must be in an eligible rural area. [3]
  • Your local Rural Development office takes applications year round. Approval times depend on funding in your area. [3]

Section 504 Home Repair loans and grants

USDA Rural Development

Loans and grants for homeowners with low or very-low income in eligible rural areas, including to make a home accessible for a household member with a disability.

Medicaid home and community-based services waivers

If the person gets Medicaid, a home and community-based services (HCBS) waiver may pay for changes to the home. Each state builds its own waivers and chooses the services they offer. [4]

  • HCBS waivers serve people who prefer to get long-term care services and supports at home or in the community. A person must need a level of care that would meet the state’s rules for services in an institutional setting. [4]
  • Besides standard services such as personal care and respite care, states can propose other services that help people move from institutions into their homes and community, or stay out of them. [4]
  • Michigan’s MI Choice waiver, for people 65 or older and adults 18 to 64 with physical disabilities who meet a nursing facility level of care, lists environmental accessibility adaptations among its services. It is one example. [5]
  • Each state chooses the most people a waiver will serve, so ask whether there is a wait. [4]

In Ohio? Follow the Ohio waiver home modification checklist. Your state’s Medicaid agency is listed on your state’s page and under Benefits and health coverage.

Area Agencies on Aging and the Eldercare Locator

Area Agencies on Aging coordinate and offer services that help older adults stay in their homes, if that is what they want. [6]

  • An Area Agency on Aging is a public or private nonprofit agency that a state names to serve older people in one area: a city, a county or several counties. Local names vary. [6]
  • Under the federal Older Americans Act rules, the in-home services these programs support include minor home modifications that help an older person stay independent and healthy, when another program doesn’t cover them. [7]

Eldercare Locator

Administration for Community Living

A free public service of the Administration for Community Living that connects older adults and their families to services. Call or text trained staff, chat online, or send an email.

Next step: Give your ZIP code or your city and state, and ask for your Area Agency on Aging and whether it helps with home modifications or repairs.

State assistive technology programs

Every state, D.C., Puerto Rico and the U.S. territories has a federally funded assistive technology (AT) program. [9]

  • AT programs let people become familiar with specific devices through demonstrations, borrow devices for a limited time, and get reused equipment. [9]
  • Each state’s AT program includes financing activities, which help people buy assistive technology through loans and other means. [9]

Your state assistive technology program

Taxes: home changes as a medical expense

If you pay for changes yourself, amounts you pay for special equipment installed in a home, or for improvements, can count as medical expenses when their main purpose is medical care for you, your spouse or your dependent. [10]

  • Certain improvements made to accommodate a home to your disability, or that of your spouse or dependents who live with you, don’t usually increase the home’s value, so their cost can be included in full. [10]
  • IRS examples include entrance or exit ramps, widening doorways and hallways, railings, support bars or other bathroom changes, lowering kitchen cabinets, moving electrical outlets, modifying stairways, handrails or grab bars anywhere, door hardware and grading the ground for access. [10]
  • Porch lifts and other lifts are on the list too, but elevators generally add value to a house. [10]
  • When an improvement does raise the property’s value, only its cost minus the increase in value counts as a medical expense. [10]
  • Only reasonable costs to accommodate a home to a disability count as medical care. Amounts you pay to operate and keep up the improvement can also qualify. [10]
  • The deduction covers only the part of your medical and dental expenses above 7.5% of your adjusted gross income, and is claimed on Schedule A as an itemized deduction. [10]

If you rent

Federal fair housing rules let renters with disabilities make reasonable changes to their home at their own expense. [11]

  • A landlord may not refuse to let you make reasonable modifications, at your expense, if they may be necessary for your full enjoyment of the home. [11]
  • Where it is reasonable to do so, the landlord may require you to agree to restore the inside to its earlier condition, except for reasonable wear and tear. [11]
  • The landlord may not raise the usual security deposit for people with disabilities. [11]

Quick answers

Who pays for a wheelchair ramp at home?

It depends on your situation. Veterans can ask VA about HISA and the SAH and SHA grants. Some state Medicaid waivers pay for home changes. USDA lends and grants money to low-income homeowners in rural areas, and Area Agencies on Aging may help older adults with minor modifications. [1][2][5][3][7]

How much is the USDA home repair grant?

Up to $10,000, for very-low-income homeowners 62 or older, to remove health and safety hazards or make a home accessible. Loans go up to $40,000 at 1 percent interest over 20 years, and a loan and a grant can be combined for up to $50,000. [3]

How much does VA pay for home modifications?

HISA has a lifetime limit of $6,800 for a service-connected disability, or for a veteran rated at least 50 percent service-connected, and $2,000 otherwise. For fiscal year 2027, the SAH grant is up to $133,042 and the SHA grant up to $26,654. [1][2]

Are home modifications tax deductible?

They can count as a medical expense. Ramps, wider doorways, bathroom grab bars and porch lifts usually don’t add value to a home, so their full cost can count, but only medical expenses above 7.5% of your adjusted gross income are deductible, on Schedule A. [10]

Can my landlord stop me from putting in a ramp?

Under federal fair housing rules, a landlord may not refuse reasonable modifications you pay for if they may be necessary for your full enjoyment of the home. Where reasonable, the landlord may ask you to restore the inside when you leave. [11]
