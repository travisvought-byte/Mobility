Deaf and hard of hearing resources in every state

15 national services and 158 state and local programs for people who are deaf, hard of hearing or DeafBlind, in 51 states and territories. Each listing says how to reach the program, links to its own page and, where the program says so, who it serves and how to start.

In crisis or unsafe right now?

Call 988 or text 988, any time. Deaf or hard of hearing: call 988 from your videophone, text 988, or dial 711 and then 988. In immediate danger, call 911.

How to use this page

  1. Start with the national services below. Most are free and work wherever you live.
  2. Then find your state for the agencies and programs there.
  3. Open “Who it serves and how to start” on any listing to see what the program itself says about who qualifies and the first step.
Before you call: what to have ready and what to ask

Have ready

  • A recent eye exam or hearing test, if you have one.
  • What has become hard to do: reading mail, using the phone, getting around, working.
  • Proof of where you live and of income, if the program asks.
  • How you want to be contacted: voice, text, email, videophone or relay.

Ask

  • Do you come to my home, or do I travel to you?
  • Is the equipment free, loaned or sold?
  • Who can sign the form that confirms my vision or hearing loss?
  • Is there training for my family too?

These are general reminders. Each program sets its own rules, so ask what it needs.

711 relay: phone calls for people who are deaf, hard of hearing or have a speech disability

For: Deaf or hard of hearing

Federal Communications Commission consumer guide

Dial 711 from any telephone anywhere in the United States to reach a Telecommunications Relay Service communications assistant, who relays the call between you and the person you are calling. The FCC describes it as fast, functional and free. Voice callers can dial 711 to call someone who uses a TTY.

Next step: Dial 711, then give the number you want to call. You can use it to call any phone number listed in this directory.

Good to know
  • 711 does not work for Video Relay Service, IP Relay or IP captioned telephone service; those start online or through your provider.
  • In an emergency, TTY users should call 911 directly instead of going through 711.

911 and Text-to-911: reaching emergency help when you cannot make a voice call

For: Deaf or hard of hearing

Federal Communications Commission consumer guide

In an emergency, the FCC says to make a voice call to 911 if you can, because a call gives the 911 center more information. Texting 911 works only in certain areas. If you text 911 where it is not available, your phone company must send back an automatic message telling you to reach 911 another way.

Next step: If you are deaf, hard of hearing or have a speech disability and text-to-911 is not available where you are, the FCC says to use a TTY or a relay service if possible. The FCC keeps a list of areas that accept texts; check yours before you need it.

Good to know
  • Each 911 call center decides whether to accept texts.

Captioned telephone service (IP CTS): read captions of what the other person says

For: Deaf or hard of hearing, Older adults

Federal Communications Commission consumer guide

Captioned telephone service lets a person with hearing loss who can use their own voice, and has some remaining hearing, talk on the phone while reading captions of what the other person says. The internet-based version (IP CTS) works on a captioned phone with a screen, and there are versions for smartphones and computers. The FCC says the relay costs are paid from the federal relay fund, so users do not pay for the captioning.

Next step: Read the FCC guide, then ask your audiologist, your state phone equipment program or your state relay program how to sign up with a captioned telephone provider.

Good to know
  • Federal rules limit the service to people with hearing loss who need captions to use the phone, and providers ask you to register.
  • Ask the provider or your state program whether the phone itself costs anything.

ADI accredits nonprofit programs that train assistance dogs. Its member search lists accredited programs by area and shows which ones work with veterans or will consider working with a person’s own dog. ADI itself does not provide, train or certify dogs.
