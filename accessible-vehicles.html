Before you buy: the order that saves money

An evaluation tells you what equipment you need. Check with every program that might help pay before you spend anything, because some must approve first.

Five steps, in this order

  1. Get a driving evaluation from a driver rehabilitation specialist. It looks at your vision, strength, coordination, judgment and ability to drive with the equipment, and you should get a written report with recommendations. [1][2]
  2. Ask every program that might help pay before you buy. VA, for one, must approve the vehicle or equipment first. [1][3]
  3. Choose a mobility dealer. Check that it is registered with NHTSA, and check its credentials, training and warranty. NMEDA advises confirming the seller is accredited under its Quality Assurance Program (QAP). [1][2]
  4. Get on-the-road training and practice with a driver rehabilitation specialist before you drive with new equipment. [1]
  5. Send the automaker its mobility reimbursement application, if it has one, to get part of the cost back. [2]
  • NHTSA says a new vehicle modified with adaptive equipment can cost $20,000 to $80,000. Some equipment, like a seat-back cushion, can cost as little as $50, and hand controls can cost under $1,000. [1]
  • Vocational rehabilitation and workers’ compensation agencies may help pay for a driver evaluation, and health insurance may pay part or all of it. [1]

VA help for veterans and service members

VA can pay toward a specially equipped vehicle and for adaptive equipment when a service-connected disability qualifies. [3]

  • Effective October 1, 2026, VA may pay up to $27,908.90 toward a specially equipped vehicle. The seller files the claim with VA, and VA makes a one-time payment directly to the seller. [5]
  • You need a service-connected disability, or one VA treats as service-connected, that includes at least one of these: loss, or permanent loss of use, of one or both feet or hands; permanent decreased vision in both eyes; a severe burn injury; or ALS. [3]
  • Ankylosis (a stiff, fused joint) in one or both knees or hips qualifies for the adaptive-equipment grant only, not the vehicle payment. [3]
  • Adaptive-equipment grants may cover features like power steering, brakes, seats, windows, or lift equipment to help you get into and out of the vehicle. [3]
  • File and get VA’s approval before you buy. For the vehicle payment, apply online or mail VA Form 21-4502. For adaptive equipment, VA says to take VA Form 10-1394 to the prosthetic representative at your local VA medical center. [3]
  • Adaptive equipment must be prescribed by a VA doctor or a Certified Driver Rehabilitation Specialist. VA’s equipment schedule also sets labor rates for installing, repairing, reinstalling and replacing it. [6]
  • A second vehicle payment may be possible if you bought a vehicle with the first one 30 or more years ago, or if a natural disaster destroyed it through no fault of yours and insurance did not already cover the loss. [3]
  • VA trains veterans and service members to drive their adapted vehicle through the VA Driver’s Rehabilitation Program. [6]

Vocational rehabilitation: when you need the vehicle for work

Your state vocational rehabilitation (VR) agency may help pay part or all of a vehicle modification. [1]

  • Federal VR rules treat vehicle modification as a rehabilitation technology service. They can count buying or repairing a vehicle, including a van, as transportation you need to take part in VR services. [7]
  • There are 78 state VR agencies. In 22 states, people who are blind or have low vision are served by a separate agency for the blind. [8]
  • If you get SSI, or would qualify except for your income or savings, a Plan to Achieve Self-Support (PASS) lets you set money aside for a work goal without it counting against SSI. Transportation is one of the things a plan can pay for. Apply with form SSA-545-BK; Social Security can give you the number of a PASS specialist. [9]

Medicaid waivers that pay for vehicle changes

Some state Medicaid programs for care at home list vehicle modifications as a service. [10]

  • Medicaid home and community-based services (HCBS) waivers serve people who prefer to get long-term care at home or in the community. Each state builds its own, and states can propose services beyond the usual list. [11]
  • Michigan’s MI Choice waiver, for people 65 or older and adults with physical disabilities who meet a nursing facility level of care, lists vehicle modifications among its services. It is one example. [10]
  • Each state chooses the most people a waiver will serve. [11]

Ask your state Medicaid agency, or your waiver case manager, whether a waiver you are in or could join covers vehicle modifications. Your state’s Medicaid agency is on your state’s page, and the next-step finder shows sourced vehicle routes for some states.

Automaker rebates for adaptive equipment

Most major automakers repay part of the cost of adaptive equipment, usually up to $1,000. [1]

  • NHTSA says the rebates are typically for vehicles less than one year old. [1]
  • NMEDA’s list shows most automakers offering up to $1,000. BMW and MINI list up to $2,500, and Audi up to $1,500 for hand controls or other approved devices. Some cover only new vehicles; some also cover certified pre-owned ones. [12]
  • Each program has its own rules, so contact the manufacturer with questions. [12]

Low-cost loans, savings and tax help

If programs will not cover it all, these can lower what you pay.

  • Every state, D.C., Puerto Rico and the U.S. territories has a federally funded assistive technology (AT) program. Its state financing work helps people buy assistive technology through financial loans and other means. [13]
  • An ABLE account lets a person whose disability began before age 46 save money. Transportation is one of the expenses it can pay for, and up to $100,000 in the account does not count as a resource for SSI. [14]
  • On federal taxes, the cost of special hand controls and other special equipment installed in a car for a person with a disability can count as a medical expense. So can the difference in cost between a regular car and a car specially designed to hold a wheelchair. [15]
  • Only the part of your medical and dental expenses above 7.5% of your adjusted gross income counts, and the deduction is claimed on Schedule A as an itemized deduction. [15]
  • Some states waive sales tax on adaptive devices when you have a doctor’s prescription for them. [1]
  • Workers’ compensation typically covers adaptive equipment needed because of a job-related injury. Auto insurance may cover all or part of it when the need comes from a crash. [1]

Finding a dealer and checking the vehicle

Not every installer works to the same standard, so check before you buy. [2]

  • NMEDA says some installers do not follow federal safety standards or third-party accreditation, and advises confirming the seller is QAP accredited before you buy. [2]
  • NHTSA keeps a search of registered dealers and modifiers. [1]
  • Before buying a new vehicle, sit in it first. Check getting in and out, how well you can see, and its crash and rollover ratings. [1]
  • Regular maintenance keeps the vehicle and its adaptive equipment safe and reliable. [1]

Quick answers

Does the VA pay for a wheelchair accessible van?

VA may pay up to $27,908.90 toward a specially equipped vehicle, effective October 1, 2026, for a veteran or service member whose service-connected disability qualifies. It is a one-time payment made to the seller. VA can also pay for adaptive equipment. Apply and get approval before you buy. [5][3]

Will Medicaid pay for vehicle modifications?

Some state Medicaid home and community-based services waivers list vehicle modifications as a service, such as Michigan’s MI Choice waiver. It depends on your state and your waiver, so ask your state Medicaid agency or your case manager. [10][11]

How much does a wheelchair accessible vehicle cost?

NHTSA says a new vehicle modified with adaptive equipment can cost $20,000 to $80,000. Smaller changes cost less: hand controls can cost under $1,000. [1]

Do car companies give rebates for adaptive equipment?

Yes. Most major automakers offer rebates on adaptive equipment, usually up to $1,000, typically for vehicles less than a year old. A few offer more: BMW and MINI list up to $2,500 and Audi up to $1,500. [1][12]

Can vocational rehabilitation help pay for a vehicle?

It can. NHTSA says to contact your state vocational rehabilitation agency about help paying for a modification. Federal VR rules treat vehicle modification as a rehabilitation technology service and can count buying or repairing a vehicle as transportation needed for VR services. [1][7]

Can I deduct the cost of a wheelchair van on my taxes?

Part of it may count. The cost of special equipment installed in a car for a person with a disability, and the extra cost of a car specially designed to hold a wheelchair, can be medical expenses. Only medical expenses above 7.5% of your adjusted gross income count, as an itemized deduction. [15]
