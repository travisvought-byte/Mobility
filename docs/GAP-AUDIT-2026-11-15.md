# Gap audit: Mobility Talk from the seat of a person looking for help

Reviewed November 15, 2026. The question was simple: if a person with a disability, anywhere in the United States, opens this site looking for help, what do they find, and what is missing?

This is a desk review of the site and its data, with automated accessibility checks in a real browser. It is not a formal accessibility audit and no one who uses assistive technology has tested the site yet.

## How the review was done

1. Read every page and every script, and ran the full test suite to set a baseline.
2. Loaded each page type in a browser at desktop, phone (390 px) and small-phone (320 px) widths, and ran axe-core (WCAG 2.0, 2.1 and 2.2 A and AA rules, plus best practices).
3. Counted the directory by need, by state and by who a program is for, and looked for whole kinds of help that were absent.
4. For each gap, read the official source and added records only from what that page showed.

## What was found

### 1. The front door spoke only to wheelchair users

The data already held state agencies for blind residents, talking-book libraries, deaf-blind equipment, phone equipment for hearing loss and vocational rehabilitation. The home page did not say so. Its first words were about a broken wheelchair and its nine starting choices were all about mobility equipment and access. A person who is blind, deaf or has a psychiatric or developmental disability had no reason to think the site was for them.

### 2. Programs were filed where their users would not look

- 542 records sat under "Aging and disability navigation", including every blind agency, talking-book library and deaf-blind program. That group had no button on the directory page.
- All 51 vocational rehabilitation agencies sat under "Coverage and funding".
- There was no way to ask "what is here for someone who is blind?" or "for a child?"

### 4. The directory opened on Ohio for everyone

A visitor from Arizona who pressed "Find programs near me" saw 172 Ohio programs until they noticed the State box.

### 5. Thirteen Pennsylvania statewide programs were filed under a county named "Pennsylvania"

The state's Protection and Advocacy agency, its assistive technology library, its aging and disability first-call line and ten others disappeared as soon as a visitor chose a real Pennsylvania county. They also could not appear on the printed help list.

### 6. Phone numbers with no relay note

The directory is built around phone numbers, with no word on how a deaf or hard of hearing person, or a person with a speech disability, can use them.

### 7. Accessibility defects found by the automated checks

- Home page map: the visible two-letter code was not part of each link's accessible name, so a voice-control user saying "click OH" got nothing (WCAG 2.5.3, 51 links).
- State pages and the not-found page skipped a heading level.
- Links on state pages opened new tabs without saying so, and every link was labeled "Official page" even when it led to a directory.

### 8. No page said who runs the site, how it is sourced, or how to report a barrier

That information was split across footers. There was no accessibility statement.

## What this change does

**A front door for every disability.** The home page now opens with an urgent-help strip, then "What do you need help with?": twelve needs and twelve "who is it for" choices, with a state box that carries the choice into every link. Nothing is stored. The equipment and access finder stays, directly below.

**Six new kinds of help in the directory**, each with a button: crisis, safety and mental health; income, benefits and health coverage; in-home help, food and daily living; housing; work, school and training; vision, hearing and communication. Existing records moved to where their users look: blind agencies, talking-book libraries, relay and phone-equipment programs into vision and hearing; vocational rehabilitation into work.

**A "Who is it for?" filter.** Records carry tags for twelve groups (blind or low vision, Deaf or hard of hearing, DeafBlind, mobility, intellectual or developmental disability, autism, brain injury, mental health, progressive conditions, children and families, older adults, veterans). Untagged records serve everyone.

**162 new records, each read from its source on November 15, 2026:**

- 46 national records, among them 988, Text-to-911, Crisis Text Line, the Veterans Crisis Line, the domestic violence hotline and its ASL line, the Disaster Distress Helpline, the Disability and Disaster Hotline, SAMHSA and NAMI helplines, 211, the federal DIAL database, Social Security (apply and appeal), HealthCare.gov, ABLE accounts, SNAP, Lifeline, respite and paid family caregiving, HUD rental help, the Job Accommodation Network, the EEOC, Parent Centers, 711 relay, the FCC ASL line, services for blind and deaf people, The Arc, the Autism Society, Easterseals, condition helplines, legal aid, complaint offices for health care and schools, voting rights and Amtrak.
- A Medicaid agency for every state, D.C. and all five territories, from the Medicaid.gov list.
- An Adult Protective Services reporting line for every state and D.C., plus Guam, from the National Adult Protective Services Association list. That list shows no statewide number for Colorado, North Carolina or Wisconsin; those three entries say so and give another route.
- Protection and Advocacy agencies for Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands, American Samoa and the Northern Mariana Islands, and the Native American Disability Law Center for Arizona, New Mexico and Utah, from the National Disability Rights Network list.

**What to have ready and what to ask.** Every kind of help now opens with a short "Before you call" list, on the directory and on the plain state pages.

**A know-your-rights page.** Thirteen situations (work, public places, housing, school, health care, transit, air travel, phones, voting, benefit denials, abuse, agency problems, and where to get help with any of them): who handles it, first steps, who to contact, and the deadline where the source states one. Phone numbers come straight from the directory records, so the two cannot drift apart.

**No default state.** With no state chosen the directory shows national resources and asks for a state. Links that name a state work as before.

**Territories.** Five territory pages and a territories group in the state box. Listings are few and the pages say so, pointing to the federal database that lists the rest.

**An about page** covering who runs the site, sourcing, privacy, what has and has not been done for accessibility, and how to report a barrier. Every footer links to it.

**Fixes.** The Pennsylvania scope error (corrected in the extraction, so it cannot recur for any state), map link names, heading order, honest link labels and new-tab notices on state pages, a relay note beside every list of phone numbers, focus moved to the results after a need is chosen, and no sideways scrolling down to 320 px.

## What is still missing

Listed in the order a visitor is most likely to feel it. None of these was added, because none could be read from a source to the standard this directory keeps in the time available.

1. **Disabled parking placards and plates.** Rules sit with each state motor vehicle agency (and in a few states with local offices). Needs one record per state from each state's own page.
2. **Vehicle modification.** The finder has this barrier but the directory has almost nothing: NHTSA guidance, dealer and driver-evaluation locators, VA automobile and adaptive equipment benefits.
3. **Blind services in the 32 states without a separate blind agency.** Their older-blind and low-vision programs sit inside general rehabilitation agencies and are not listed by name.
4. **State offices for deaf and hard of hearing people.** Only a few states have an entry.
5. **State mental health agencies and local NAMI affiliates.** National lines only so far.
6. **Early intervention (birth to three) and Family-to-Family Health Information Centers** in each state. Parent Centers are listed nationally, not state by state.
7. **Utility shut-off protection and energy assistance, and home-delivered meals.**
8. **American Job Centers** and tribal vocational rehabilitation programs.
9. **Housing beyond the HUD line:** state housing finance agencies, Section 811, accessible-unit search tools, emergency shelter.
10. **Territories beyond Medicaid and rights agencies:** independent living centers, assistive technology programs, councils, rehabilitation agencies. Not in the printed help list or the letter builder.
11. **Spanish.** The site is in English only. A first step would be one page of the national lines that answer in Spanish.
12. **The next-step finder still covers mobility only.** Widening it to communication access, service animals, school and work would need the same law-by-law review the current claims had.
13. **Twelve states have no rural transit entry and seven have no phone-equipment entry.** Some of those states may have no such program; each needs a look.

## Verification limits

- Automated checks found no violations on any page type at 1280, 390 and 320 px. Automated checks find only part of what a person would. The earlier recommendation stands: test with five to eight disabled users, including keyboard, switch, screen reader and voice-control users and people with cognitive or fatigue-related access needs.
- New records were read from their source pages once. They were not confirmed by phone, in keeping with the site's practice.
- State lists (Medicaid, Adult Protective Services) are only as current as the national pages they came from. Each record names its source.
- "Before you call" lists are general reminders written for this site, not rules of any program.
