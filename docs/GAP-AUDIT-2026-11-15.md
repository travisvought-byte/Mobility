# Gap audit: Mobility Talk from the seat of a person looking for help

Reviewed November 15, 2026. The question was simple: if a person with a disability, anywhere in the United States, opens this site looking for help, what do they find, and what is missing?

This is a desk review of the site and its data, with automated accessibility checks in a real browser. It is not a formal accessibility audit and no one who uses assistive technology has tested the site yet.

## How the review was done

1. Read every page and every script, and ran the full test suite to set a baseline.
2. Loaded each page type in a browser at desktop, phone (390 px) and small-phone (320 px) widths, and ran axe-core (WCAG 2.0, 2.1 and 2.2 A and AA rules, plus best practices).
3. Counted the directory by need, by state and by who a program is for, and looked for whole kinds of help that were absent.
4. For each gap, read the official source and added records only from what that page showed.

## What was found

### 1. The front door spoke only to wheelchair users

The data already held state agencies for blind residents, talking-book libraries, deaf-blind equipment, phone equipment for hearing loss and vocational rehabilitation. The home page did not say so. Its first words were about a broken wheelchair and its nine starting choices were all about mobility equipment and access. A person who is blind, deaf or has a psychiatric or developmental disability had no reason to think the site was for them.

### 2. Programs were filed where their users would not look

- 542 records sat under "Aging and disability navigation", including every blind agency, talking-book library and deaf-blind program. That group had no button on the directory page.
- All 51 vocational rehabilitation agencies sat under "Coverage and funding".
- There was no way to ask "what is here for someone who is blind?" or "for a child?"

### 3. Whole kinds of help were missing

| Need | Before this review |
| --- | --- |
| Crisis lines (988, text, Deaf access, veterans) | None |
| Reporting abuse, neglect or exploitation | A handful of local entries in six states |
| Applying for Social Security disability, and appealing | None |
| Applying for Medicaid | None |
| Mental health help | None outside developmental disability offices |
| Finding housing | Home modification only |
| Job accommodations and job discrimination | None |
| School rights and parent help | None |
| Food, phone and internet help | None |
| Paid family caregiving and respite | None as its own route |
| Voting | None |
| Health care discrimination | None |
| Legal aid outside a few states | None nationally |
| U.S. territories | None |
| Native American rights agency | None |

### 4. The directory opened on Ohio for everyone

A visitor from Arizona who pressed "Find programs near me" saw 172 Ohio programs until they noticed the State box.

### 5. Thirteen Pennsylvania statewide programs were filed under a county named "Pennsylvania"

The state's Protection and Advocacy agency, its assistive technology library, its aging and disability first-call line and ten others disappeared as soon as a visitor chose a real Pennsylvania county. They also could not appear on the printed help list.

### 6. Phone numbers with no relay note

The directory is built around phone numbers, with no word on how a deaf or hard of hearing person, or a person with a speech disability, can use them.

### 7. Accessibility defects found by the automated checks

- Home page map: the visible two-letter code was not part of each link's accessible name, so a voice-control user saying "click OH" got nothing (WCAG 2.5.3, 51 links).
- State pages and the not-found page skipped a heading level.
- Links on state pages opened new tabs without saying so, and every link was labeled "Official page" even when it led to a directory.

### 8. No page said who runs the site, how it is sourced, or how to report a barrier

That information was split across footers. There was no accessibility statement.

## What this change does

**A front door for every disability.** The home page now opens with an urgent-help strip, then "What do you need help with?": twelve needs and twelve "who is it for" choices, with a state box that carries the choice into every link. Nothing is stored. The equipment and access finder stays, directly below.

**Six new kinds of help in the directory**, each with a button: crisis, safety and mental health; income, benefits and health coverage; in-home help, food and daily living; housing; work, school and training; vision, hearing and communication. Existing records moved to where their users look: blind agencies, talking-book libraries, relay and phone-equipment programs into vision and hearing; vocational rehabilitation into work.

**A "Who is it for?" filter.** Records carry tags for twelve groups (blind or low vision, Deaf or hard of hearing, DeafBlind, mobility, intellectual or developmental disability, autism, brain injury, mental health, progressive conditions, children and families, older adults, veterans). Untagged records serve everyone.

**162 new records, each read from its source on November 15, 2026:**

- 46 national records, among them 988, Text-to-911, Crisis Text Line, the Veterans Crisis Line, the domestic violence hotline and its ASL line, the Disaster Distress Helpline, the Disability and Disaster Hotline, SAMHSA and NAMI helplines, 211, the federal DIAL database, Social Security (apply and appeal), HealthCare.gov, ABLE accounts, SNAP, Lifeline, respite and paid family caregiving, HUD rental help, the Job Accommodation Network, the EEOC, Parent Centers, 711 relay, the FCC ASL line, services for blind and deaf people, The Arc, the Autism Society, Easterseals, condition helplines, legal aid, complaint offices for health care and schools, voting rights and Amtrak.
- A Medicaid agency for every state, D.C. and all five territories, from the Medicaid.gov list.
- An Adult Protective Services reporting line for every state and D.C., plus Guam, from the National Adult Protective Services Association list. That list shows no statewide number for Colorado, North Carolina or Wisconsin; those three entries say so and give another route.
- Protection and Advocacy agencies for Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands, American Samoa and the Northern Mariana Islands, and the Native American Disability Law Center for Arizona, New Mexico and Utah, from the National Disability Rights Network list.

**What to have ready and what to ask.** Every kind of help now opens with a short "Before you call" list, on the directory and on the plain state pages.

**A know-your-rights page.** Thirteen situations (work, public places, housing, school, health care, transit, air travel, phones, voting, benefit denials, abuse, agency problems, and where to get help with any of them): who handles it, first steps, who to contact, and the deadline where the source states one. Phone numbers come straight from the directory records, so the two cannot drift apart.

**No default state.** With no state chosen the directory shows national resources and asks for a state. Links that name a state work as before.

**Territories.** Five territory pages and a territories group in the state box. Listings are few and the pages say so, pointing to the federal database that lists the rest.

**An about page** covering who runs the site, sourcing, privacy, what has and has not been done for accessibility, and how to report a barrier. Every footer links to it.

**Fixes.** The Pennsylvania scope error (corrected in the extraction, so it cannot recur for any state), map link names, heading order, honest link labels and new-tab notices on state pages, a relay note beside every list of phone numbers, focus moved to the results after a need is chosen, and no sideways scrolling down to 320 px.

## Round two, later the same day

The first round listed thirteen gaps. This round filled the ones that could be read from official pages without phoning anyone.

**Early intervention in every state and territory.** 56 records, one for each state, D.C. and the five territories, from the ECTA Center's list of state programs for babies and toddlers (birth to age three). Where the list gives a referral line the record shows it; where it gives only the state office, the record says so and links the state's own referral page when there is one. Each record says what to ask for, that the evaluation is free and that no referral is needed, as stated by the Center for Parent Information and Resources. The list was read twice, independently, and the two readings matched for all 56 places.

**A new kind of help, "Help for a child"** (babies, children and school), holding early intervention and the Parent Centers. It has its own button on the directory, its own "Before you call" list and a card on the home page. The know-your-rights page gained a fourteenth situation: a baby or toddler who may need help.

**19 national records:**

- Energy: LIHEAP heating and cooling help, state utility shut-off rules, the Weatherization Assistance Program.
- Food: the National Hunger Hotline, home-delivered meals through the Area Agencies on Aging (the entry says plainly that the federal program is for people 60 and older, with limited exceptions), and USDA food programs for older adults.
- Housing: emergency shelter (211 and HUD's Find Shelter tool), HUD-approved housing counselors, Non-Elderly Disabled vouchers, the National Call Center for Homeless Veterans, the National Runaway Safeline.
- Work: American Job Centers, the federal hiring path for people with disabilities, AbilityOne, Veteran Readiness and Employment.
- Driving and parking: NHTSA's guidance on adapting a vehicle, the ADED directory of driver rehabilitation specialists, and how disabled parking placards work (a federal guideline, with each state issuing its own).
- The national early intervention overview, which steps aside once a state is chosen.

**Territories.** Assistive technology programs for all five territories (AT3 Center list) and vocational rehabilitation agencies for Puerto Rico and the U.S. Virgin Islands (AskJAN list). With early intervention, each territory page now lists four or five programs in place of two or three.

**Texas vocational rehabilitation** now has a phone number, from the AskJAN list.

**A Spanish page**, `espanol.html`: twelve national lines and resources whose own pages say they serve Spanish speakers, the English sentence to ask for an interpreter, and links back to the English directory. Every number on it is pulled from a directory record. Every English page links to it. The Spanish was written for this site and has not yet been reviewed by a fluent speaker; the page says so.

**Home page.** Sixteen need cards in place of twelve: help for a child, support groups, everything in my state (which goes straight to the chosen state's page) and Ayuda en español. On phones, card titles no longer break short words with hyphens.

## Round three, the same night

**A Family-to-Family Health Information Center in every state and territory.** 60 listings and a national entry: one for each state, D.C. and the five territories, a second center in Minnesota and New Mexico, and the centers for tribal communities listed for Alaska and Oklahoma. These are family-led centers, funded by the federal Maternal and Child Health Bureau, that help families of children and youth with special health care needs at no cost. Each listing was read from that state's page in the Family Voices locator. Every page was read twice, and a number is shown only where both readings agree. Where the locator shows only a contact number in place of an office line (Ohio, Puerto Rico and two tribal centers), the listing says so. They sit under "Help for a child" beside early intervention.

**VA automobile allowance and adaptive equipment**, read from VA's own page and application form: who qualifies, how to apply, and the instruction not to buy until VA has authorized the purchase.

**State agencies for people who are deaf or hard of hearing.** 45 listings from the national roster kept by the association of these agencies. The roster gives names and links but no phone numbers, so each listing points to the agency's own page and says plainly that the roster shows no phone. The roster's links for Illinois and Indiana point to Idaho's site, so those two listings carry no link. Eighteen links are older addresses, which the listings flag. Pennsylvania's office was already listed from the state's own page.

## Round four, overnight

**Disability parking placards in every state and D.C.** 51 listings, one per state and D.C., each read from that state motor vehicle agency's own page and its current application form. Each names the form, who may certify the disability, where to send it, and the temporary-placard rules and fees where the agency states them, and ends with a reminder to check the agency's page before applying. Sixteen carry a phone number, each one the agency showed on its page or form; the rest say how to reach the agency through its page. New York's listing explains that permits come from the local city, town or village clerk rather than the state DMV. South Carolina's agency had no page for this that could be read, so its listing links the state's application form, a Word document, and says so. Where a state offers something a disabled driver may not know about, the listing mentions it: Maryland's reserved residential space and hidden-disability symbol, Kansas's cognitive disorder placard, Kentucky's communication designation, Pennsylvania's reserved sign near home, Nevada's expedited service permit and others. With a state chosen, the national parking entry gives way to the state listing; it still shows for the territories, which have no state listing yet.

## Round five, overnight

**Help lines and peer support for specific conditions.** A person who has just been told they or their child has a particular condition usually looks first for the national group that knows it. Twenty-four national organizations were added, each read from its own website: the ALS Association, Arthritis Foundation, Spina Bifida Association, Sickle Cell Disease Association of America, National Down Syndrome Society, NORD (rare diseases), the Cystic Fibrosis Foundation's Compass service, United Cerebral Palsy, Little People of America, the GBS|CIDP Foundation, the Huntington's Disease Society of America, the Depression and Bipolar Support Alliance, Cure SMA, the CMT Association, Post-Polio Health International (with the International Ventilator Users Network), the Hydrocephalus Association, the OI Foundation, the National Ataxia Foundation, the American Cancer Society (its 24-hour line and, separately, its free rides to treatment), the American Lung Association's Lung HelpLine, the National Kidney Foundation's NKF Cares, CHADD's National Resource Center on ADHD, the Tourette Association of America and the Lupus Foundation of America. Each says who answers, when, what the line will and will not do (several are clear that they give no medical advice), and how to find a local group. Where a site printed a lettered number such as 800-345-HDSA, the listing stores the digits so the call button works and keeps the lettered form in the note.

**Other national supports.** Supported decision-making, the less restrictive alternative to guardianship, with the national resource center's state-by-state pages. Donated Dental Services, free dental care from volunteer dentists for people who are 65 or older, permanently disabled or need dental care for a medical reason, and cannot pay, with its limits (use any coverage first, a waiting list, once only). Assistance Dogs International's search for accredited service dog programs, with the note that ADI does not certify dogs and that VA's service dog benefit requires an accredited program.

**Two corrections to existing entries.** The energy-bill entry now carries the federal referral line (NEAR, 1-866-674-6327, weekdays 9 to 7 Eastern) from the federal LIHEAP page, and a warning that LIHEAP never charges a fee to apply. The service animal entry now says staff may not ask for paperwork, a demonstration or about the disability, that online certificates give no rights under the ADA, and when a dog may be asked to leave.

## Round six, early on November 16

**Help at home for older adults losing their vision.** Most people who lose their sight do so after 55, and every state receives federal money for independent living services for them: training in using remaining vision, magnifiers and other devices, phones and computers, staying safe at home and getting around. The directory had these programs for only three states. Thirty-six states and D.C. now have a listing, each read from that state's page on Time To Be Bold, the consumer site of the national technical assistance center for these programs at Mississippi State University. Each says who runs the program, who qualifies and how to apply where the state page says so, and gives the program's line where the page lists one (29 do); where the page lists only local offices or providers, the listing says so and links the page. Staff names and personal email addresses on those pages were left out. Every listing notes that the federal program is meant for people who are not looking for work, and that Hadley offers free lessons if there is a waiting list. A national entry points to the same site and gives way wherever a state listing exists. Michigan, Ohio and Texas already had their programs and were not duplicated.

**Nine more condition organizations,** each read from its own website: the Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America (counselor helpline and peer phone support), the Prader-Willi Syndrome Association (a 24-hour family support line for medical and behavioral emergencies), the National Fragile X Foundation, the Cerebral Palsy Foundation, the National Aphasia Association (email only, as its site lists no phone), the International Rett Syndrome Foundation, the Angelman Syndrome Foundation, the Williams Syndrome Association and Parent Project Muscular Dystrophy.

**Parent Centers, again.** The national Parent Center map answered a few requests and then refused the rest for over an hour, so the state-by-state list is still not finished. What was read (Alabama, Alaska, Arkansas, California, Colorado and Ohio) is saved for the next attempt. The federal grantee list on ed.gov hides its tables from the reader used here.

## Round seven, November 16

**Parent Centers in 40 states.** A parent whose child is struggling at school usually needs one thing first: someone who knows special education and is on the family's side. Every state has at least one federally funded Parent Center for this. The national map of these centers kept refusing repeated requests, so most centers were read instead from their own websites, through search results limited to each center's own site; six states (Alaska, Arkansas, California, Colorado, Connecticut and Delaware) come from the map itself, read one state at a time. Each listing says what the center says about itself (for example, that it is the state's federally funded Parent Training and Information Center), gives a phone number only where the center's own page or the map showed one, and notes that Parent Centers give information and support, not legal representation. Illinois's listing explains that the state's center changed on October 1, 2025. Where a number came from an older document on the center's site (Louisiana, 2019), the listing says so. The national Parent Centers entry now gives way wherever a state has its own listing.

## Round fifteen, November 16

**National pages for blind and low vision, and for deaf and hard of hearing.** A person searching for "services for the blind in my state" or "free phones for deaf people" had no page to land on: the programs were in the directory, which needs JavaScript, and spread across 56 state pages. `blind-low-vision.html` and `deaf-hard-of-hearing.html` now gather every record tagged for each group, national services first and then state by state, with each program's own words on who it serves and how to start. The home page carries structured data naming the site and its maker, every main page carries a breadcrumb, and the maker's name in the footer links to travisvought.com.

14. **More condition groups.** About 45 national condition and disability organizations are listed now. Long COVID has no listing yet, and many smaller condition groups (for example dysautonomia, myositis and hemophilia) are not yet read. Each needs its own site read the same way.

## Verification limits

- Automated checks found no violations on any page type at 1280, 390 and 320 px. Automated checks find only part of what a person would. The earlier recommendation stands: test with five to eight disabled users, including keyboard, switch, screen reader and voice-control users and people with cognitive or fatigue-related access needs.
- New records were read from their source pages once. They were not confirmed by phone, in keeping with the site's practice.
- State lists (Medicaid, Adult Protective Services, early intervention, family health information centers, state deaf and hard of hearing agencies, territory assistive technology and rehabilitation agencies) are only as current as the national pages they came from. Each record names its source. The early intervention list showed "Data Current as of November 14, 2026."
- The Spanish page has not been reviewed by a fluent Spanish speaker.
- "Before you call" lists are general reminders written for this site, not rules of any program.
